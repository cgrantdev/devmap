**Last verified against FDA and HHS primary sources: 2026-10-31.** Federal compounding lists, advisory votes, and enforcement priorities change. This guide is educational pathway literacy, not legal advice.

## The short version

1. **FDA approval** of a finished drug is not the same as **pharmacy compounding** permission, and neither is the same as an online **“research use only” (RUO)** listing.
2. Under **section 503A**, a bulk drug substance generally may be used in compounding only if it has an applicable USP/NF monograph, is a component of an FDA-approved drug, or appears on the **503A Bulks List** established by FDA rulemaking.
3. While that Bulks List is developed, FDA has used **interim categories**. **Category 1** is the interim bucket associated with practical enforcement discretion for compounding evaluation. **Category 2** flags significant safety-risk concerns (do-not-compound posture under the interim policy framing).
4. **Removal from Category 2 is not permission to compound.** It does not place a substance on the Bulks List and does not equal Category 1.
5. A **Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) recommendation** is **advisory**. It is **not** a final FDA rule and does **not** by itself authorize compounding.
6. As of **29 October 2026**, peptides reviewed at the **July 2026 PCAC** meeting (including BPC-157 and TB-500) **had not been added to the 503A Bulks List by FDA rulemaking**. The codified 503A Bulks List does not name them, and no proposed rule to add them has appeared in the Federal Register. Treat any “now legal to compound” claim with extreme skepticism unless it cites a final listing.
7. On **28 October 2026** the **HHS Office of the Secretary** announced a **draft interim policy**, the “Enforcement Discretion Policy Regarding 503A Compounding Using Certain Peptide Bulk Drug Substances” (FR Doc. 2026-22192; Docket No. HHS-OASH-2026-0496). The notice of availability was published in the Federal Register on **30 October 2026** at **91 FR 68526**, and Regulations.gov lists the comment period as closing on **13 November 2026**. It describes when HHS does not intend to take enforcement action against certain 503A compounding of six peptides. It is **not** FDA approval of any peptide, **not** a 503A Bulks List listing, and **not** binding. See the dated update below.

### Scope and conditions (as written; not usage guidance)

The policy lists ten conditions. In summary:

- **Listed identity and route only (Appendix B).** BPC-157 acetate (subcutaneous, intramuscular, oral, and topical dosage forms); TB-500 acetate, the fragment Ac-Leu-Lys-Lys-Thr-Glu-Thr-Gln-OH (subcutaneous or intramuscular injectable dosage forms); MOTS-c acetate (subcutaneous or intramuscular injectable dosage forms); epitalon acetate (subcutaneous or intramuscular injectable dosage forms dispensed in intermittent courses); semax free base (intranasal use); KPV acetate (topical and oral dosage forms). Other salt forms are outside the listed identities. This list describes the policy’s scope. It is not usage guidance.
- **Sourcing and testing.** The bulk substance must come from a facility registered under section 510 with a valid certificate of analysis, and the original manufacturer must be FDA-registered and CGMP-compliant, with its location listed on the certificate.
- **Reporting and labeling.** Compounders report products compounded, serious adverse events, and product quality issues to FDA and to the States, and report serious adverse events and product quality issues to FDA’s MedWatch program. Labels must include “This is a compounded drug product. Not for resale.”
- **Single ingredient.** “The compounded drug product contains only a single Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) listed in Appendix A.” Blends are outside the policy.
- **Other limits.** Oral forms need documentation of adequate oral bioavailability in humans; the only co-packaged device may be an FDA 510(k)-cleared syringe; and section 503A’s insanitary-conditions and patient-specific prescription requirements still apply.

### What it is not

- **Not an FDA approval.** The notice states: “Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, which means FDA does not review their safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed.” The documents make no safety or efficacy finding for any of the six peptides.
- **Not a 503A Bulks List listing.** None of the six is on 21 CFR 216.23(a). The notice says “FDA’s future rulemakings may propose adding some or all of these peptides to the 503A bulks list”; that rulemaking is still pending.
- **Not Category 1.** None of the six is in any FDA interim category. HHS’s Appendix B says each was in Category 2 until 22 April 2026, and FDA’s categories list updated 14 May 2026 does not name them.
- **Not binding and not permanent.** The notice says the draft policy “does not establish any rights for any person and is not binding on HHS or the public.” There is no calendar end date: it runs until a final rule addresses each peptide, and “HHS may revisit application of this interim policy (in whole or in part) if, among other things, a safety signal or product quality issue arises.”
- **Not a change to RUO status.** HHS states that the policy is meant to “discourage the continued, widespread use of certain research-use-only peptides supplied by foreign manufacturers that pose a safety risk to US patients.” Nothing in the policy treats research-use-only products as covered.
- **Advisory votes are still advisory.** For each peptide, Appendix B states that FDA’s Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) recommended against listing and that the PCAC voted in favor. FDA has not posted vote results on its meeting page, and this guide gives no vote counts.

## How 503A compounding lists actually work

Section **503A** of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act governs traditional patient-specific compounding by state-licensed pharmacies (high level). FDA’s compounding pages explain that bulk drug substances used under 503A are limited. In plain language, a substance generally needs one of these pathways:

- An applicable **USP or NF monograph**, or
- Being a **component of an FDA-approved drug**, or
- Appearing on FDA’s **503A Bulks List** (built through agency evaluation / rulemaking)

**Section 503B** covers outsourcing facilities that register with FDA and face different bulk-substance and quality rules (including a separate 503B bulks / clinical-need framework and shortage-related pathways). This guide focuses on the 503A literacy most readers mean when they ask about “compounded peptides,” without turning into a full pharmacy-law manual.

### Interim categories (why “Category 2” dominates headlines)

While formal bulks lists are developed, FDA has sorted nominated substances into interim categories. Educational explainers (including clinic/legal trackers) commonly describe:

- **Category 1** — substances under evaluation where FDA may exercise enforcement discretion; the only interim category that carries **practical** compounding consideration in that framing. Category 1 is still not FDA drug approval and is not, by itself, a license to compound.
- **Category 2** — substances FDA has identified as raising **significant safety risks**; do-not-compound posture under the interim policy
- **Category 3** — nominations lacking adequate supporting information (where that category is used)
- **Withdrawn nominations** — a separate posture: nominators withdrew a nomination. **Being “withdrawn” / off Category 2 is not permission**

Two details from FDA’s 503A pages (503A page content current as of 14 May 2026) matter for the October 2026 news. First, FDA’s guidance “does not intend to place bulk drug substances nominated on or after January 7, 2025, into these categories.” Second, FDA’s 503A categories list, updated 14 May 2026, shows GHK-Cu (except for injectable routes of administration) added back to Category 1 after a nominator clarified its withdrawal. That is an interim enforcement-discretion posture with conditions, not FDA approval and not a Bulks List listing. Injectable GHK-Cu remains in the withdrawn-nomination table.

FDA also publishes a page on bulk substances that may present significant safety risks, including substances still listed under Category 2 and a table of substances whose nominations were withdrawn (with narrative safety concerns retained for context)—including entries such as **BPC-157** and **thymosin beta-4 fragment (LKKTETQ), also known as TB-500**.

## The distinction almost everyone gets wrong

In **2026**, FDA actions that **removed certain peptides from Category 2** (including via withdrawn nominations) were widely reported as those peptides “becoming legal again.” That is not what Category 2 removal means.

- Off Category 2 ≠ on the **503A Bulks List**
- Off Category 2 ≠ **Category 1**
- Off Category 2 ≠ automatic lawful compounding under 503A

A peptide can therefore be **off Category 2** and **still not lawfully compoundable** under the interim framework—which is the situation many popular research peptides have been in while PCAC review and rulemaking continue.

## What PCAC did—and did not do—in July 2026

On **23–24 July 2026**, FDA’s **Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee** reviewed several nominated peptide bulk substances for possible **503A Bulks List** inclusion. Reporting on the meeting (including BMJ coverage and legal analyses) describes committee votes that **recommended inclusion** for multiple peptides such as **BPC-157** and **TB-500**, while FDA staff briefing materials had argued against inclusion for the set under review.

Critical literacy points:

- PCAC votes are **advisory and non-binding**
- FDA still decides whether/how to add a substance to the Bulks List
- Addition generally requires **notice-and-comment rulemaking**
- A favorable vote is **not** marketing approval as a drug
- A favorable vote is **not**, by itself, a green light to compound

## Example: BPC-157 and similar peptides (primary-supported only)

Use these as **illustrations of pathway confusion**, not as a complete legality chart.

### BPC-157

- **Not** an FDA-approved drug for human use (FDA Approved Drugs database does not list it; DoD OPSS describes it as an unapproved drug).
- Appears in FDA’s compounding safety-risk materials (including withdrawn-nomination / safety-narrative context regarding immunogenicity, peptide impurities/API characterization, and limited human safety information for proposed routes). That withdrawn / off Category 2 framing is **not** “placed in Category 1.”
- **July 2026 PCAC** reporting: committee recommended 503A Bulks List inclusion; **recommendation ≠ final listing**.
- **WADA 2026 Prohibited List**: named under **S0 Non-approved substances** (prohibited at all times)—**sport rules**, not a substitute for FDA compounding analysis.
- DoD OPSS: listed among prohibited dietary supplement ingredients for service members; research-chemical labeling does not change that workplace guidance.
- Encyclopedia illustration only: [BPC-157](/encyclopedia/BPC-157).

### TB-500 (thymosin β4 fragment LKKTETQ)

- **Not** an FDA-approved drug for injectable injury use.
- FDA safety-risk materials discuss the **fragment** and note lack of identified human exposure data for that fragment in FDA’s summary.
- July 2026 PCAC reporting: recommended for inclusion (advisory).
- **WADA**: thymosin-β4 and derivatives **e.g. TB-500** named under **S2.3** (prohibited at all times).

## What “research use only” means—and does not

**RUO / “not for human consumption”** is a labeling and marketing posture. Legitimate laboratories do need research reagents. That is real.

What RUO does **not** automatically do:

- Create FDA approval
- Place a substance on a compounding bulks list
- Guarantee purity, sterility, or correct identity
- Erase FDA’s ability to evaluate **intended use** from the totality of marketing

Under **21 CFR 201.128**, “intended uses” are based on objective intent—expressions, design/composition, and circumstances of distribution—including labeling claims and advertising. FDA warning letters to peptide sellers have treated website disease/structure-function style claims as evidence that products are being marketed as **drugs**, and then as **unapproved new drugs** when no approval is in effect—regardless of research branding.

The **24 August 2026** FDA warning letter to Peptide Partners LLC says this directly about that firm’s labeled products: despite statements marketing them “for research use only” and “not for human or veterinary use,” website evidence established that the products were intended to be drugs for human use. RUO wording on those products did not prevent the unapproved-new-drug finding in that letter. This is an enforcement example, not a complete list of every August 2026 letter and not legal advice about any other seller.

**High-level takeaway:** legality depends on pathway and marketing context, not on a disclaimer sticker. Static “legal or illegal” charts go stale quickly; prefer FDA primary pages and dated trackers over slogans.

## WADA / sport (briefly)

Anti-doping rules are **not** general criminal law and are **not** FDA compounding law. Athletes subject to testing follow the **WADA Prohibited List** (and their sport’s program). As of the **2026** list:

- **BPC-157** — S0 (named example)
- **Thymosin-β4 and derivatives e.g. TB-500** — S2.3

A blend containing a prohibited substance is still a sport risk. Check the current list; it updates annually.

## Practical literacy checklist (not legal advice)

1. Is there an **FDA-approved finished drug** for this exact product/use?
2. If compounded: does it qualify under **503A/503B** pathways **today** (monograph, approved-drug component, or final bulks listing—not vibes)? Category 1 is interim enforcement discretion, not a qualifying pathway or a listing.
3. Was a headline about **Category 2 removal** or a **PCAC vote** being treated as if it were a final rule?
4. Is an online seller making **drug claims** while waving an RUO disclaimer?
5. Do **sport / DoD / employer** rules separately restrict the substance?
6. When in doubt: **current FDA pages + qualified counsel**—not forums.

## FAQs

### Are research peptides legal to buy online?

There is no single yes/no answer for every peptide, seller, claim, and jurisdiction. RUO listings exist; FDA still polices unapproved new drugs marketed for human use. This page does not advise purchases.

### Does “removed from Category 2” mean pharmacies can compound it?

No. Removal from Category 2 is not placement on the 503A Bulks List and is not Category 1 permission.

### Did PCAC “approve” BPC-157 or TB-500 in July 2026?

No. PCAC votes are non-binding recommendations. FDA’s briefing documents proposed that BPC-157 and TB-500 not be included, and the HHS policy’s Appendix B states that the committee voted in favor. A vote is not FDA drug approval and not a final bulks-list rule.

### Did FDA approve compounded peptides in October 2026?

No. On 28 October 2026 the HHS Office of the Secretary announced a draft interim enforcement-discretion policy published for comment (FR Doc. 2026-22192). It describes when HHS does not intend to take action against certain 503A compounding of six peptides under listed conditions. It is not FDA drug approval, it does not place any peptide on the 503A Bulks List or in Category 1, it is not binding, and it makes no safety or efficacy finding. FDA rulemaking on the Bulks List is still pending.

### Is compounding the same as FDA approval?

No. Compounding is a limited pharmacy pathway. Approval is a separate product authorization process.

### Does WADA ban mean something is illegal for everyone?

No. WADA governs athletes in anti-doping programs. It is separate from FDA compounding and general consumer law—though the same substance can be constrained in multiple systems at once.

### Where should I check status before believing a clinic ad?

FDA compounding / bulks / safety-risk pages first; then dated educational trackers; then counsel. On-site Peptidemap posts are context only—cross-check against primary FDA sources when making decisions.
