From 2022 into 2025, FDA listed semaglutide and tirzepatide injection products as in shortage. While a drug is on that list, federal law gives compounders more room to make versions of it. Once FDA found the shortages resolved, that room closed after set grace periods. Two lawsuits challenged the decisions and lost. In 2026, FDA turned to marketing claims, bulk ingredients and the 503B bulks list.

This post sets out that timeline with dates from FDA pages, court opinions and the Federal Register. It also explains what each legal status means for a product: approved, compounded and research-labelled.

### Tirzepatide: the removal, the remand and the 19 December 2024 decision

FDA first said on 2 October 2024 that the tirzepatide injection shortage was resolved. Compounders sued. FDA's 22 October 2024 update said the decision "has been remanded to the agency for reevaluation." The Fifth Circuit opinion describes this as a voluntary remand and stay that the district court granted. On 19 December 2024, FDA issued a declaratory order to Eli Lilly titled "Resolution of Shortages of Tirzepatide Injection Products (Mounjaro and Zepbound)." The order "revokes and replaces FDA's October 2, 2024 decision" and finds the shortage resolved.

### Semaglutide: 21 February 2025

On 21 February 2025, FDA said it "has determined the shortage of semaglutide injection products … is resolved." It said it had confirmed with Novo Nordisk that its "stated product availability and manufacturing capacity can meet the present and projected national demand." The determination was also issued as a declaratory order to Novo Nordisk, covering Ozempic and Wegovy.

FDA's notices about both drugs warned that patients and prescribers may still see intermittent, localized supply disruptions. That does not change the national determination.

## The grace periods FDA set for 503A and 503B

FDA did not enforce right away. For each drug it said it did not intend to act against compounders "for violations of the FD&C Act arising from conditions that depend on" the drug's inclusion on the shortage list until set dates. It also said it could still act on other violations, such as substandard or unsafe products.

## What compounding rules apply after a shortage ends

Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. They are exempt from approval only if they meet the conditions in section 503A or 503B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. FDA's 1 April 2026 reminder summarizes the conditions that matter for GLP-1 drugs.

### Section 503A: "essentially a copy"

- A 503A drug must be compounded for an individual patient based on a prescription.
- The compounder may not compound, "regularly or in inordinate amounts," drugs that are essentially copies of a commercially available drug product.
- FDA says that when a drug shortage is resolved, it "generally considers the drug to be commercially available."
- Under FDA guidance, a compounded drug is essentially a copy if it has the same active ingredient in the same, similar or easily substitutable strength, and the approved drug can be used by the same route. The exception is when a prescriber determines and documents a change that produces a significant difference for an identified patient.
- FDA's example is a product that combines semaglutide with another ingredient, such as vitamin B12. It may still count as essentially a copy if it is given the same way and each ingredient's amount is within 10% of the approved products' strengths.
- FDA states that, for now, it does not intend to act on the "regularly or in inordinate amounts" condition if a compounder fills four or fewer prescriptions of such a product in a calendar month.

### Section 503B: bulks list or shortage list

- An outsourcing facility cannot compound from bulk drug substances unless the substance is on the 503B bulks list, which covers substances with a clinical need, or the compounded drug is on FDA's drug shortage list at the time of compounding, distribution and dispensing.
- FDA states: "Tirzepatide and semaglutide do not currently appear on the 503B bulks list or on FDA's drug shortage list."
- Section 503B also bars essentially copies of approved drugs. FDA explains this as meaning the compounded drug "may not be identical or nearly identical to an FDA-approved drug unless the approved drug is on FDA's drug shortage list."

### The 503B bulks list proposal

On 30 April 2026, FDA announced it was proposing to exclude semaglutide, tirzepatide and liraglutide from the 503B bulks list, "finding no clinical need for outsourcing facilities to compound these drugs from bulk substances." The Federal Register notice (91 FR 23431, 1 May 2026) says FDA "tentatively finds no basis to conclude that there is a clinical need." After an extension notice on 26 June 2026, the comment period closed on 30 July 2026. A Federal Register search on 19 October 2026 found no final determination. **Status: proposed, not final.**

## What FDA's "concerns with unapproved GLP-1 drugs" page says

FDA keeps a page titled "FDA's Concerns with Unapproved GLP-1 Drugs Used for Weight Loss" (content current as of 1 October 2026). It says unapproved versions, compounded ones included, "do not undergo FDA's review for safety, effectiveness and quality before they are marketed." Its main points, described here and not offered as advice:

- **Dosing errors.** FDA "received multiple reports of adverse events, some requiring hospitalization, that may be related to dosing errors associated with compounded injectable semaglutide products." These came from patients measuring and self-administering incorrect amounts and, in some cases, from health care professionals miscalculating.
- **Doses beyond the label.** FDA also received reports that may be related to compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide prescribed beyond the approved label. That covers larger single doses, more frequent doses and faster titration. Reported symptoms included nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain and constipation.
- **Adverse event counts.** As of 31 May 2026, FDA had received 990 adverse event reports associated with compounded semaglutide and more than 730 associated with compounded tirzepatide. FDA notes that state-licensed pharmacies that are not outsourcing facilities are not required to report adverse events, so these are "likely" underreported. It also says causation cannot always be determined.
- **Salt forms.** FDA says semaglutide sodium and semaglutide acetate are different active ingredients from the one in the approved drugs, and that it is "not aware of any lawful basis for their use in compounding."
- **Fraudulent labels and quality.** The page describes fraudulent compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide labels naming pharmacies that do not exist or did not make the product. It also covers complaints about products arriving warm, concerns about multiple-dose vials, and an import alert (66-80) on GLP-1 active ingredients with potential quality concerns.
- **Substances that cannot be compounded.** FDA states that retatrutide and cagrilintide "cannot be used in compounding under federal law."
- **Research labels.** FDA says it has warned companies that sold semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide, survodutide or mazdutide "falsely labeled 'for research purposes' or 'not for human consumption'" directly to consumers for human use.

## Litigation: OFA v. FDA

The Outsourcing Facilities Association and other compounders brought two cases in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas. Only outcomes confirmed in FDA updates or court opinions are listed here.

- **Tirzepatide (No. 4:24-cv-00953).** The preliminary injunction was denied on 5 March 2025. The Fifth Circuit later found the compounders had not made a "clear showing" of likely success. The district court entered summary judgment for FDA and Eli Lilly. On 27 August 2026, the Fifth Circuit affirmed (No. 25-10600, unpublished). It held that any notice-and-comment error was harmless because no prejudice was shown, and that the shortage determination was not arbitrary or capricious.
- **Semaglutide (No. 4:25-cv-00174).** The preliminary injunction was denied on 24 April 2025, and the court entered summary judgment for FDA and Novo Nordisk on 13 June 2025. On 27 August 2026, the Fifth Circuit affirmed "for the same reasons" (No. 25-10758, unpublished).

Unpublished Fifth Circuit opinions are generally not binding precedent under that court's Rule 47.5. Both rulings still leave the delisting decisions in place. No rehearing or certiorari petition had been filed as of 19 October 2026.

## 2026 FDA actions on GLP-1 compounding

- **6 February 2026.** In a statement, FDA announced its "intent to take decisive steps to restrict GLP-1 active pharmaceutical ingredients" intended for non-approved compounded drugs mass-marketed as similar alternatives to approved drugs. It named one company and referred to other compounding pharmacies. FDA said promotions cannot call compounded products generic versions or the same as approved drugs, and it cited possible "seizure and injunction."
- **3 March 2026.** FDA announced 30 warning letters to telehealth companies over false or misleading claims about compounded GLP-1 products. The main violations were implied sameness with approved drugs and branding that implied the telehealth firm was the compounder.
- **1 April 2026.** FDA added its reminder on the 503A and 503B conditions, described above, to its GLP-1 compounding page.
- **30 April and 1 May 2026.** FDA proposed excluding semaglutide, tirzepatide and liraglutide from the 503B bulks list. This is still a proposal.
- **8 June 2026.** FDA's warning-letter database lists further letters dated 8 June 2026 to telehealth firms. They cite false or misleading claims about compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide under sections 502(a) and 502(bb).

FDA's guidance page for telehealth companies lists the claims it treats as false or misleading. They include calling a compounded drug generic or the same as an approved drug, claiming FDA approval or evaluation, and claiming a product comes from an "FDA-approved" pharmacy. FDA notes that it does not approve or license any facility.

As of 19 October 2026, FDA's shortage data list liraglutide injection as a current shortage. No current shortage entry was found for semaglutide or tirzepatide injection.

## Approved, compounded and RUO-labelled: what each status means

| Status | What it legally means | FDA review before marketing | GLP-1 position as of 19 October 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FDA-approved drug | A specific product approved under an NDA for its labelled uses | Yes: safety, effectiveness and manufacturing quality | Approved semaglutide and tirzepatide products include Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus, Mounjaro and Zepbound |
| Compounded under 503A | A drug made by a state-licensed pharmacy or physician for an identified patient on prescription, meeting 503A conditions | No | Shortage-based room ended in 2025. The essentially-a-copy limit applies, with FDA's stated four-prescriptions-a-month enforcement position |
| Compounded under 503B | A drug made by an FDA-registered outsourcing facility that meets 503B conditions | No. Facilities register with FDA, but their products are not approved | Neither drug is on the 503B bulks list or the shortage list. An exclusion from the bulks list is proposed |

## What this does not settle

- **Individual-patient compounding.** Whether a specific 503A prescription qualifies depends on facts such as a documented significant difference and state pharmacy law. This post does not assess any case.
- **The final 503B bulks decision.** FDA's exclusion is a proposal until it publishes a final determination.
- **State law.** State boards of pharmacy have their own rules. This post covers federal status only.
- **Product quality.** None of the statuses above says whether a given product is good or bad. Questions about treatment belong with a licensed clinician and pharmacist.

## FAQ

### When did FDA resolve the tirzepatide and semaglutide shortages?

FDA first announced the tirzepatide injection shortage resolved on 2 October 2024. After litigation, it re-evaluated that decision and issued a declaratory order on 19 December 2024 confirming the shortage was resolved. FDA determined the semaglutide injection shortage resolved on 21 February 2025.

### What grace periods did FDA give compounders?

For tirzepatide, FDA said it would not act on shortage-dependent violations until 18 February 2025 for 503A pharmacies and physicians and 19 March 2025 for 503B outsourcing facilities, later tied to a court ruling. The 503A period ended when the court denied a preliminary injunction on 5 March 2025. For semaglutide, the dates were 22 April 2025 for 503A and 22 May 2025 for 503B. The 503A period ended when a preliminary injunction was denied on 24 April 2025.

### Does the end of a shortage end all compounding of these drugs?

Not entirely, but it removes the shortage pathway. Under 503A, a pharmacy may not compound essentially a copy of a commercially available drug regularly or in inordinate amounts, and FDA generally treats a drug as commercially available once its shortage is resolved. Under 503B, outsourcing facilities cannot compound from bulk substances unless the substance is on the 503B bulks list or the drug is on the shortage list, and FDA says tirzepatide and semaglutide are on neither.

### What happened in the OFA v. FDA lawsuits?

Compounders challenged both delisting decisions in the Northern District of Texas. The court denied preliminary injunctions in the tirzepatide case on 5 March 2025 and the semaglutide case on 24 April 2025, then entered summary judgment for FDA and the manufacturers. On 27 August 2026 the Fifth Circuit affirmed both judgments in unpublished opinions.

### What has FDA done on GLP-1 compounding in 2026?

On 6 February 2026 FDA announced its intent to restrict GLP-1 active ingredients intended for mass-marketed compounded drugs. On 3 March 2026 it announced 30 warning letters to telehealth companies over compounded GLP-1 claims. On 30 April 2026 it proposed not to include semaglutide, tirzepatide and liraglutide on the 503B bulks list; the comment period closed on 30 July 2026 and no final determination was found as of 19 October 2026.

### What does FDA say about dosing errors and adverse events?

FDA's page on unapproved GLP-1 drugs says it received adverse event reports, some involving hospitalization, that may be related to dosing errors with compounded injectable semaglutide, and reports tied to doses beyond the approved labels. As of 31 May 2026 it had received 990 reports for compounded semaglutide and more than 730 for compounded tirzepatide. FDA notes these are likely underreported and causation is not always clear.

### Is research-labelled semaglutide or tirzepatide the same as compounded?

No. Compounding is a legal pathway with conditions under sections 503A and 503B. A research-use-only label is not a compounding pathway or an approval. FDA says it has warned companies that sold semaglutide, tirzepatide and other GLP-1 drugs falsely labelled for research purposes or not for human consumption.
