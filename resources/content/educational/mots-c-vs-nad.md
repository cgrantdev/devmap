MOTS-c and NAD+ often show up side by side in longevity and "mitochondrial health" listings. They are very different things. MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded in mitochondrial DNA. NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a coenzyme found in every cell. It is not a peptide. Being listed together does not make them alternatives, and we found no study comparing or combining them in searches of PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov on 19 October 2026.

This article covers what each molecule is, how each is thought to work, how strong the evidence is, and each one's US regulatory status, with dates. Animal-only findings are labelled. For NAD+, trials of the precursors nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) are kept apart from studies of NAD+ itself.

Peptidemap is an RUO / grey-area peptide comparison publisher, not a clinic, pharmacy or supplier. This article is educational. It is not medical advice, gives no dosing, and does not rank one molecule above the other.

## What each is

| | MOTS-c | NAD+ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Class | Peptide (16 amino acids) | Dinucleotide coenzyme (not a peptide) |
| Where it comes from | A short open reading frame in the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene | Made in all tissues from dietary niacin forms and the amino acid tryptophan |
| Human interventional trials published | None identified | Many precursor trials; few small studies of NAD+ itself |
| FDA-approved drug containing it | None | None identified |
| 503A compounding status (19 October 2026) | Not on the 503A Bulks List; previously Category 2, nomination withdrawn | NAD and NADH in 503A Category 1 (under evaluation) |

**MOTS-c.** MOTS-c stands for "mitochondrial open reading frame of the 12S rRNA-c." The founding paper (Lee et al., *Cell Metabolism* 2015) described it as a 16-amino-acid peptide that "regulates insulin sensitivity and metabolic homeostasis," with skeletal muscle as its apparent primary target organ. FDA's July 2026 briefing document notes that MOTS-c "is a common name and not a United States Adopted Name," that it has no USP or NF monograph, and that it is not a component of any FDA-approved drug.

**NAD+.** The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements describes NAD as the main metabolically active form of niacin (vitamin B3). It states that "more than 400 enzymes require NAD to catalyze reactions in the body." Beyond carrying electrons in energy metabolism, NAD+ is consumed by signalling enzymes. A 2018 review in *Cell Metabolism* names the sirtuins and PARPs as the two main NAD+-responsive signalling families, and calls the enzyme CD38 "a major consumer of NAD+ in mammals."

Peptidemap's compound pages are [MOTS-c](https://peptidemap.com/encyclopedia/mots-c) and [NAD+](https://peptidemap.com/encyclopedia/nad).

### NAD+ precursors vs direct NAD+

Most human NAD+ studies do not give NAD+ itself. They give a precursor that cells convert into NAD+.

- **NR and NMN** are the precursors with the most recent human trials. NIH ODS lists NR among the niacin-related derivatives.
- **NAD+ itself** has very few human studies. NIH ODS notes that NAD and NADP eaten in food "are converted to nicotinamide in the gut and then absorbed." Human data on directly given NAD+ come mainly from one small intravenous pharmacokinetic pilot.

A result for NR or NMN is not a result for NAD+. Research-market "NAD+" vials are a different product from the NR and NMN capsules used in most trials.

## How the evidence is graded here

- **Human RCT:** randomized, placebo-controlled human trial reporting a clinical or physiological outcome.
- **Small human:** pilot, crossover or observational human study, or a randomized trial with biomarker-only outcomes or a few dozen participants or fewer.
- **Animal/cell (animal-only):** mice, rats or cultured cells. These results show biological plausibility. They do not show effects in people.

## MOTS-c: proposed mechanisms and evidence

### Proposed mechanism (animal/cell)

- **Folate cycle and AMPK (cell and mouse).** Lee et al. reported that MOTS-c inhibits the folate cycle and the de novo purine synthesis linked to it. This leads to accumulation of the metabolite AICAR and activation of AMPK, a cellular energy sensor.
- **Nuclear signalling (cell).** Kim et al. (*Cell Metabolism* 2018) reported that under metabolic stress MOTS-c enters the nucleus, AMPK-dependently, and regulates antioxidant-response genes alongside factors such as NRF2.
- **Target still unknown.** FDA's briefing states that "the molecular target(s) through which MOTS-c acts remains unknown," which makes it "difficult to predict which organs might be targeted."

### Animal findings (animal-only)

- **Diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance (mouse).** In the 2015 paper, MOTS-c "prevented age-dependent and high-fat-diet-induced insulin resistance, as well as diet-induced obesity" in mice. FDA's briefing summarizes later mouse work reporting lower blood glucose in obese mice and less weight gain after ovariectomy.
- **Physical capacity and late-life treatment (mouse).** Reynolds et al. (*Nature Communications* 2021) reported that MOTS-c "can significantly enhance physical performance in young (2 mo.), middle-age (12 mo.), and old (22 mo.) mice." Treatment started late in life increased physical capacity and healthspan in mice. The overall lifespan curve showed only a non-significant trend toward longer median and maximum lifespan (log-rank P = 0.23); the authors say larger cohorts are needed. FDA's briefing reports the medians as 912 days with vehicle and 970 days with MOTS-c. Two of the paper's authors, Cohen and Lee, disclose that they are consultants and shareholders of CohBar, the company behind CB4211 (see below).
- **Bone and blood vessels (rodent and cell).** FDA also summarizes reports of effects on bone remodelling and on vascular calcification in a rat model.
- **Limits FDA points out.** These studies "have been limited to in-vivo and in-vitro rodent models," and "the clinical relevance of the findings of these nonclinical studies is currently unknown." FDA identified no toxicity studies.

### Human data (small human and observational only)

None of the human studies below gave MOTS-c to anyone. They measured the body's own MOTS-c.

- **Endogenous MOTS-c and exercise (small human).** In Reynolds et al., 10 sedentary, healthy young men cycled on a stationary bike. The authors report that "exercise induces endogenous MOTS-c expression in skeletal muscle and in circulation."
- **A second exercise study (small human).** von Walden et al. (*Journal of Applied Physiology* 2021) randomized 30 people to endurance exercise, resistance exercise or a control condition. Circulating humanin rose after endurance exercise. MOTS-c "showed a trend to increase" that did not reach significance.
- **A genetic variant (observational).** Zempo et al. (*Aging* 2021) studied m.1382A>C, an Asian-specific variant that changes one amino acid in MOTS-c (K14Q). Across three cohorts (27,527 people), men but not women carrying the C-allele had a higher prevalence of type 2 diabetes. This is a genetic association, not evidence about giving MOTS-c.
- **Measurement problems (analytical).** A doping-control lab (Knoop et al. 2019) found that levels measured in 20 healthy people with a commercial ELISA kit "could not be confirmed" by mass spectrometry. FDA cites the same group's finding that MOTS-c breaks down quickly in human blood in vitro, which "raises the question as to whether exogenous administration of MOTS-c to humans can generate pharmacologically active concentrations."

### Human trials of MOTS-c itself

- **Published:** none identified. FDA's briefing memo (dated 11 May 2026) states FDA "did not identify clinical studies evaluating administration of MOTS-c-related BDSs in human subjects." Its search of the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System through 9 March 2025 "retrieved no reports."
- **Registered:** [NCT07505745](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07505745) is a phase 2a, randomized, placebo-controlled trial of MOTS-c in adults with prediabetes and overweight or obesity, sponsored by Hudson Biotech at a site in China. It is listed as recruiting (estimated 120 participants, primary completion February 2027), with insulin sensitivity and adverse events as primary outcomes. No results are posted.
- **Not counted:** a completed phase 1a/1b trial of CohBar's CB4211 ([NCT03998514](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03998514)) has no results posted. The trial record does not say how CB4211 relates to MOTS-c. CB4211 is a different molecule, so its results would not be MOTS-c results.

**Grade for MOTS-c:** animal/cell for every proposed effect, plus small and observational human data on the body's own MOTS-c. There are no human RCTs of MOTS-c.

## NAD+: proposed mechanisms and evidence

### Proposed mechanism

NAD+ is required for redox reactions (established biochemistry) and is used up by sirtuins, PARPs and CD38. The "NAD+ decline" hypothesis holds that falling NAD+ with age limits these enzymes, and that restoring it could restore function. The 2018 *Cell Metabolism* review reports that restoring NAD+ "in old or diseased animals can promote health and extend lifespan" (animal-only). The human picture is less clear. A 2025 *Nature Metabolism* review found that an age-related fall in NAD+ in humans "has been consistently observed only in a limited number of studies," and that "the extrapolation of rodent-based data is not straightforward."

### Precursor trials in humans (NR and NMN)

- **NR in healthy middle-aged and older adults (small human, RCT crossover).** Martens et al. (*Nature Communications* 2018) randomized 30 people to 6-week periods of NR and placebo; 24 completed. NR was well tolerated and raised NAD+ metabolism. Blood pressure and arterial stiffness were flagged for future trials, not shown.
- **NR in obese, insulin-resistant men (human RCT).** Dollerup et al. (*American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* 2018) randomized 40 men to NR or placebo for 12 weeks. Insulin sensitivity, energy expenditure and body composition did not improve. No serious adverse events were attributed to NR.
- **NR in aged men (small human, RCT crossover).** Elhassan et al. (*Cell Reports* 2019) gave 12 older men NR or placebo for 21 days. NR raised the NAD+ metabolome in muscle and lowered several circulating inflammatory cytokines. It did not change mitochondrial bioenergetics.
- **NMN in women with prediabetes (human RCT).** Yoshino et al. (*Science* 2021) randomized 25 postmenopausal women with prediabetes and overweight or obesity (13 NMN, 12 placebo) for 10 weeks. Muscle insulin sensitivity, measured by clamp, improved with NMN and did not change with placebo. It is one small trial.
- **NR in Parkinson disease, phase 1 (small human RCT).** In the NADPARK study (Brakedal et al., *Cell Metabolism* 2022), 30 newly diagnosed patients received NR or placebo for 30 days. NR raised brain NAD+ variably. The authors describe "mild clinical improvement" only in those whose brain NAD+ rose, a subgroup finding.
- **NR in Parkinson disease, phase 3 (human RCT).** The NOPARK trial (Brakedal et al., *JAMA*, published 8 October 2026) randomized 410 patients with early Parkinson disease to NR or placebo for 52 weeks, with 393 in the primary analysis. The primary outcome was change in total MDS-UPDRS (parts I–III) at week 52. In the primary-analysis population (393), the adjusted mean difference (mixed model for repeated measures) was 2.72 points (95% CI 0.47 to 4.98; P = .02) favouring placebo. That is smaller than the trial's prespecified clinically important worsening of 5.2 points. Of 5 key secondary outcomes, 4 showed no difference; non-motor symptom burden (NMSS) worsened more with NR. Serious adverse events (safety population): 8.3% with NR vs 14.1% with placebo. NR is an NAD+ precursor, so this is not a trial of NAD+ itself.
- **Muscle in older adults (meta-analysis).** Prokopidis et al. (*Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle* 2025) pooled randomized trials in adults with mean ages of 60.9 to 83 years. NMN had no significant effect on skeletal muscle index, handgrip strength, gait speed or chair-stand time.
- **The wider evidence base (systematic review).** Gallagher and Emmanuel (*Ageing Research Reviews* 2026) identified 33 human intervention studies, 28 of them randomized. Oral NR and NMN "consistently demonstrated biochemical target engagement" and were generally well tolerated over weeks to months. Functional, metabolic and vascular outcomes were "heterogeneous and often null or endpoint-specific."

### Direct NAD+ in humans

- **Intravenous pilot (small human).** Grant et al. (*Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience* 2019) studied 11 men, 8 who received an intravenous NAD+ infusion and 3 controls. For the first two hours plasma NAD+ did not rise; the authors concluded it was "rapidly and completely removed from the plasma." Only pharmacokinetics were measured. One author lists an affiliation with "NAD+ Research Inc." and a wellness center.
- **Outcome trials.** The 2026 systematic review found "no eligible outcomes trials" of intravenous or intramuscular NAD+ for anti-aging or wellness indications. It treated the intravenous pilot as "contextual evidence only."

**Grade for NAD+:** biomarker effects of oral NR and NMN are supported by human RCTs. Clinical and functional outcomes are mixed, mostly null; in one phase 3 trial the primary outcome modestly favoured placebo, by less than that trial's prespecified clinically important difference. Direct NAD+ has only small pharmacokinetic human data and no outcome trials.

## Regulatory and RUO status

For how approval, compounding categories and RUO labelling differ, see [Are Research Peptides Legal? FDA, RUO & Compounding Explained](https://peptidemap.com/guides/peptide-legality-fda-ruo-compounding).

### MOTS-c

- **29 September 2023:** FDA added MOTS-c to 503A Category 2 (significant safety risks), per its safety-risks page as captured by the Internet Archive on 3 October 2024 (a capture of fda.gov). FDA cited possible immunogenicity, peptide impurities and characterization problems, and no human exposure data.
- **14 April 2026:** Regulations.gov posted the nominator's withdrawal. FDA's safety-risks page (current as of 22 April 2026) now lists MOTS-c among substances "previously in category 2" whose nominations "were withdrawn by the nominators," with the same safety text. The 503A categories list (updated 14 May 2026) puts MOTS-c in neither Category 1 nor 2. Leaving Category 2 is not permission to compound.
- **11 May 2026:** FDA's evaluation memo, published in the briefing package, proposed "not adding MOTS-c (free base) or MOTS-c acetate to the 503A Bulks List." It found both forms "not well-characterized," with no human safety or effectiveness data. FDA reviewed MOTS-c on its own initiative after the withdrawal.
- **23 July 2026:** The Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) voted separately on MOTS-c free base and MOTS-c acetate, for the uses FDA evaluated: obesity and osteoporosis. The BMJ reported that the committee recommended six peptides, including MOTS-c, each by a narrow majority. FDA's briefing introduction says it does not intend to issue a final determination until that process and its reviews are complete; a recommendation does not change the list.
- **As of 19 October 2026:** the codified 503A Bulks List (21 CFR 216.23) does not include MOTS-c. On 14 October 2026, OMB received an HHS/FDA notice titled "Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides" (RIN 0910-ZD94). It is pending review, and its text is not public, so it is not known whether it covers MOTS-c.
- **Sport:** FDA's briefing notes that MOTS-c appears as prohibited in the Global DRO database, drawn from the 2024 World Anti-Doping Agency list.

### NAD+ and its precursors

- **NAD and NADH (compounding):** FDA's 503A categories list (updated 14 May 2026) places "Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD)" and "Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Disodium Reduced (NADH)" in Category 1, "Bulk Drug Substances Under Evaluation." Category 1 is interim enforcement discretion for 503A compounders, not FDA approval and not a Bulks List listing. A salt form, "Beta-Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Disodium Salt Trihydrate," sits in Category 3 (inadequate support), so the exact form matters. The 503B categories list for outsourcing facilities (updated 21 March 2025) places 'Beta-Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide' and 'Nicotinamide riboside chloride' in 503B Category 1, and 'Beta-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide disodium salt, trihydrate' in Category 3.
- **NAD+ (approval):** a search of FDA's Drugs@FDA data through openFDA on 19 October 2026 found no approved drug listing NAD+ (nadide) as an active ingredient.
- **NR (food use):** FDA closed GRAS Notice 635 for nicotinamide riboside chloride on 3 August 2016 with a "no questions" letter. That notice covers use as a vitamin B3 source in certain foods. It is not a drug approval.
- **NMN (supplement status):** in a letter of 11 October 2022, FDA said NMN was excluded from the dietary supplement definition because it had been authorized for investigation as a new drug. In a letter signed and posted on 29 September 2025 (docket FDA-2023-P-0872; the letter's printed date line reads 2029), answering a citizen petition, FDA concluded that "NMN is not excluded from the definition of dietary supplement," because it was sold as a supplement first. That reverses the 11 October 2022 letter. It addresses the drug-exclusion clause only: it is not a safety finding or a drug approval, and new dietary ingredient requirements still apply.

**The RUO context.** Research-labelled MOTS-c or NAD+ vials are not compounded drugs, not dietary supplements and not FDA-approved products. A "research use only" label is not a regulatory pathway for use in people.

## How they compare in research

**They answer different questions.** MOTS-c research asks whether a signal encoded in mitochondrial DNA regulates metabolism elsewhere in the body. NAD+ research asks whether topping up a coenzyme pool every cell already uses changes function, ageing or disease.

**The evidence is at different stages.**

| Evidence type | MOTS-c | NAD+ (precursors) | NAD+ (direct) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Human RCTs, clinical outcomes | None published; one phase 2a recruiting | Several; largest is NOPARK phase 3 (primary outcome modestly favoured placebo) | None identified |
| Small or biomarker human studies | Endogenous levels and exercise only | Many; NAD+ metabolites rise consistently | One small IV pharmacokinetic pilot |
| Animal and cell | Metabolic, exercise, bone, vascular; lifespan trend not significant | Extensive | Limited |
| Main open problem | No human exposure data; unreliable assays; unknown target | Biomarker change without consistent clinical benefit | Whether infused NAD+ reaches tissues usefully |

**Where they appear to overlap.** Both are discussed in terms of energy sensing, exercise and ageing. MOTS-c research leans on AMPK, while NAD+ research leans on sirtuins and PARPs. We found no interventional study combining or comparing them in searches of PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov as of 19 October 2026. Any claim that they "work together" or "stack" has no direct test behind it.

**Who this comparison is for.** Researchers, students, journalists and regulation-watchers who need to separate these molecules in the literature and FDA records, or to check whether a claim citing "studies" means human trials, mouse experiments or biomarkers. It is not a guide to using either.

Research-market listings for both compounds are on the [MOTS-c vs NAD+ compare page](/compare/mots-c-vs-nad). A listing is not evidence of identity, quality or approval.

## What the evidence doesn't tell us

- **Whether MOTS-c does anything when given to people.** No human pharmacokinetic, safety or efficacy data have been published.
- **Which MOTS-c is being measured.** ELISA and mass-spectrometry results disagree. FDA also notes that free base and acetate forms are distinct substances and that naming is inconsistent.
- **Whether higher NAD+ metabolites mean benefit.** Precursor trials reliably raise blood markers. Clinical outcomes do not follow consistently, and in NOPARK the primary Parkinson disease score modestly favoured placebo.
- **Whether direct NAD+ behaves like the precursors.** The human data are one small pharmacokinetic pilot. No outcome trials exist.
- **Long-term safety.** Precursor trials ran weeks to a year; for MOTS-c, FDA found no toxicology studies.
- **Individual response.** Trial averages and mouse results do not predict how any one person will respond. Medical decisions belong with a licensed clinician.

## FAQ

### Is NAD+ a peptide like MOTS-c?

No. NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a coenzyme made from vitamin B3 forms and tryptophan, used by hundreds of enzymes in every cell. MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded in mitochondrial DNA. They are often listed together, but they are different kinds of molecule.

### Has MOTS-c been tested in human clinical trials?

No published trial has given MOTS-c to people. FDA's May 2026 evaluation identified no clinical studies or human exposure data. A phase 2a placebo-controlled trial in adults with prediabetes (NCT07505745) is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov as recruiting, with no results posted. Human MOTS-c data so far measure the body's own peptide, for example after exercise.

### Do NR and NMN raise NAD+ in people?

Yes, as a biomarker. Randomized trials of nicotinamide riboside and nicotinamide mononucleotide consistently show higher NAD+ metabolites in blood or muscle. A 2026 systematic review of 33 human intervention studies described this as consistent target engagement. Raising a biomarker is not the same as improving a health outcome.

### Did NAD+ precursors improve health outcomes in trials?

Results are mixed and mostly null. NR did not improve insulin sensitivity in obese men, while one small trial found NMN improved muscle insulin sensitivity in women with prediabetes. In the phase 3 NOPARK trial, published in October 2026, the primary Parkinson disease score at 52 weeks modestly favoured placebo over NR, by 2.72 points, less than the trial's prespecified clinically important worsening of 5.2 points. A 2025 meta-analysis found no benefit of NMN for muscle mass or strength in older adults.

### Is MOTS-c on FDA's Category 2 list?

Not any more. FDA added MOTS-c to 503A Category 2 on 29 September 2023. After the nominator withdrew its nomination, FDA listed it among withdrawn substances previously in Category 2 and kept its safety concerns. FDA staff proposed not adding MOTS-c to the 503A Bulks List. The July 2026 advisory committee recommended it anyway, but as of 19 October 2026 no final FDA listing was identified. Leaving Category 2 is not permission to compound.

### What is NAD+'s compounding status?

FDA's 503A categories list, updated 14 May 2026, places NAD and NADH in Category 1, meaning under evaluation. Category 1 is an interim enforcement policy for 503A compounders, not FDA approval and not a listing on the 503A Bulks List. No FDA-approved drug with NAD+ as its active ingredient was found.

### Has anyone studied MOTS-c and NAD+ together?

We found no interventional study combining or comparing them in searches of PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov as of 19 October 2026. Claims that they work together or should be stacked are not based on a direct test.

### Is research-labelled MOTS-c or NAD+ the same as a compounded or approved product?

No. A research-use-only label is not a compounding pathway, a dietary supplement status or an FDA approval. Research-labelled vials are not the NR or NMN products used in most human trials. This article is educational and is not medical advice.
