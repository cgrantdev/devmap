Catalogs often place “CJC-1295 with DAC” next to “CJC-1295 no DAC” as if both inherited the same peer-reviewed file. They do not. In the literature that named **CJC-1295**, the compound is the long-acting growth-hormone-releasing-hormone (GHRH) analog that carries a Drug Affinity Complex (DAC). The material sold as “CJC-1295 no DAC” is better identified as **Modified GRF 1-29**—a related backbone without that albumin-binding design.

Peptidemap is a RUO / grey-area peptide comparison publisher. This note is identity and evidence-boundary literacy. It is **not** medical advice, a dose, a reconstitution recipe, a “which is better” ranking, or a treatment claim.

## What DAC adds (mechanism level only)

DAC, in this construct family, is an albumin-binding design. Characterization work associated with ConjuChem’s DAC-GRF / CJC-1295 program describes a reactive maleimide-containing group intended to couple with circulating albumin after administration. Albumin conjugation changes clearance behavior relative to short GHRH fragments. That design is part of the **studied DAC molecule**, not an optional marketing footnote you can remove while keeping the same pharmacokinetics.

Removing DAC changes molecular identity. It is not a toggle that leaves “the same peptide with a shorter half-life” automatically proven by DAC papers.

Both DAC CJC-1295 and Modified GRF 1-29 sit in the **GHRH-receptor (GHRHR)** lane. That is a different receptor class from ghrelin-receptor secretagogues such as ipamorelin (GHSR-1a). Shared interest in pituitary GH output does not make the receptors or compounds interchangeable.

## Pharmacokinetics: Teichman half-life is DAC-only

Teichman et al. (2006) studied CJC-1295 as a long-acting GHRH analog in healthy adults (two randomized, placebo-controlled ascending-dose trials). PubMed lists the paper as PMID [16352683](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16352683/); the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism* record is volume 91, pages 799–805 (DOI [10.1210/jc.2005-1536](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2005-1536)). The study reports an estimated half-life of **5.8–8.1 days** after single administrations of that DAC construct, with multi-day GH and IGF-I changes under the trial’s conditions.

Those numbers belong to:

- the **DAC** construct tested,
- the healthy-adult population and assays in that paper,
- that historical study design.

They do **not** authorize quoting a multi-day human half-life for catalog “CJC-1295 no DAC” / Modified GRF 1-29. If a source does not identify the no-DAC material, the correct cell is “not established by that paper,” not an imported DAC value.

Apex’s guide makes the same evidence-boundary explicit and is cited here as a secondary identity map, not as a substitute for the primary paper.

Caveats:

- PubChem synonym lists for these CIDs are messy (vendor nicknames collide). Prefer formula, mass, and CAS with attribution over a single synonym string.
- A COA still describes a **submitted sample** and lot. Wrong mass ⇒ wrong or mislabeled material for that sample. Matching mass ⇒ identity consistent with the claimed analyte under that method—not proof of sterility, next-lot sameness, or human safety.
- Do not invent additional CAS/MW values beyond verified PubChem / primary-attributed figures.

## Blend vials: two receptor lanes, one label problem

Marketed “CJC-1295 / Ipamorelin” blend vials typically pair a **GHRH-analog** component (often labeled CJC-1295 no DAC / Mod GRF) with **ipamorelin** (a GHSR-1a secretagogue). That is two receptor lanes in one container—not proof of clinical synergy, and not a license to paste DAC-construct half-life onto the GHRH-analog half of the blend.

Identity literacy for blends:

- Read whether the GHRH-analog half is described as DAC or no-DAC / Mod GRF.
- Expect a serious blend COA to name **both** analytes (or clearly state what was assayed). A single purity number with an ambiguous “CJC” label is weak documentation.

## FAQ

### Is “CJC-1295 no DAC” the same molecule as literature CJC-1295?

No. Literature CJC-1295 in the Teichman-era PK work is the DAC construct. Catalog “no DAC” is the Modified GRF 1-29 nickname lane.

### Can I quote the 5.8–8.1-day half-life for Mod GRF?

No. That estimate is from Teichman et al. 2006 for the DAC construct. It does not transfer to no-DAC material.

### Does a blend COA only need one peptide name?

Ideally no. If two peptides are in the vial, documentation should make clear which analytes were identified and how. Ambiguous “CJC” labeling is a documentation gap.

### Which form is better?

This page does not rank them. Different identity, different evidence depth, different analytical mass. Research design depends on the hypothesis and the exact material on the certificate—not on a forum winner.

## Bottom line

Treat “CJC-1295” in papers and “CJC-1295 no DAC” on storefronts as a naming trap until the DAC status is explicit. Keep Teichman’s multi-day half-life inside the DAC construct. Use mass/identity paperwork to check whether the label matches the molecule. Keep blend vials as two-lane identity problems, not as automatic synergy claims.
