BPC-157 and GHK-Cu often sit side by side in "skin and recovery" catalog sections, and some blend vials contain both. They are unrelated molecules. BPC-157 is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide studied mostly in rodents. GHK-Cu is a copper complex of a naturally occurring tripeptide, and most of its human data are topical and cosmetic.

Peptidemap is an RUO / grey-area peptide comparison publisher, not a clinic, pharmacy, or supplier. This article is educational. It is not medical advice, does not cover dosing, protocols, or reconstitution, and does not rank the two.

## What each is

| | BPC-157 | GHK-Cu |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequence | Gly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val (GEPPPGKPADDAGLV) | Gly-His-Lys (GHK) bound to copper(II) |
| Size | 15 amino acids | 3 amino acids plus Cu²⁺ |
| Molecular formula (PubChem) | C62H98N16O22 | C14H23CuN6O4+ (complex); free GHK is C14H24N6O4 |
| Molecular weight (PubChem, average) | 1419.5 g/mol | 402.92 g/mol (complex); 340.38 g/mol (free GHK) |
| PubChem CID | [9941957](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/9941957) | [71587328](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/71587328) (prezatide copper); free GHK [73587](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/73587) |
| Other names | Bepecin, PL-14736, PLD-116 (PubChem synonyms) | Prezatide copper, copper tripeptide-1 (INCI name) |
| Origin | Synthetic; described as a fragment of a gastric juice protein ("BPC") | GHK occurs naturally in human plasma, saliva, and urine |

FDA's July 2026 briefing lists BPC-157 free base as CAS 137525-51-0 with the same formula and weight, and calls it "not well-characterized" physically and chemically. Salt forms such as acetate change the formula and mass, so a label should say which form it means. GHK-Cu has a similar problem: copper-free "GHK" is sold separately, and a 2026 review in *Pharmaceutics* (Mateescu et al.) notes the literature "frequently treats GHK-Cu as a single active ingredient" even though copper binding and free copper vary by formulation.

Peptidemap's compound hubs are [BPC-157](https://peptidemap.com/encyclopedia/BPC-157) and [GHK-Cu](https://peptidemap.com/encyclopedia/GHK-Cu).

## Proposed mechanisms (research hypotheses)

Everything below comes from cell and animal research. A proposed pathway is not proof of effect in people.

**BPC-157.** FDA's 2026 briefing summarizes the hypotheses: researchers have proposed that BPC-157 may "up-regulate the expression of growth factors and their receptors, suppress release and/or expression of inflammatory factors, induce angiogenesis, and stimulate nitric oxide synthesis." It adds that "the molecular targets for BPC-157-related substances have not been identified."

**GHK-Cu.** The two Pickart reviews cited here list Skin Biology, a copper-peptide skincare company founded by Loren Pickart, as the authors' affiliation; Pickart (1938–2023) first isolated GHK in 1973. Those reviews propose that the complex supports collagen and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, modulates matrix-degrading enzymes and their inhibitors, and shifts expression of large gene sets. The independent 2026 Mateescu review says preclinical data "consistently support" effects on matrix remodeling, epithelial repair, inflammatory and redox signaling, and angiogenesis, but "the clinical evidence remains sparse."

## Evidence base side by side

| Evidence layer | BPC-157 | GHK-Cu |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell and animal | Large rodent literature (GI, tendon, ligament, muscle) | Fibroblast, wound, and gene-expression work; animal wound models |
| Human, topical | Not the main research line | Small cosmetic and dermatology studies with mixed results |
| Human, injected or systemic | A few small uncontrolled pilot reports | No published controlled trial identified |
| Systematic review signal | 2025 review: 36 studies, 35 preclinical and 1 clinical | 2026 review: clinical evidence "sparse" |
| FDA-approved drug | No | No |

**BPC-157 human data.** A 2025 systematic review in *HSS Journal* (Vasireddi et al.) included 36 studies (35 preclinical, 1 clinical) and found no clinical safety data. A 2025 narrative review (McGuire et al.) called human data "extremely limited," citing three pilot studies. Examples are an uncontrolled 12-patient interstitial cystitis series and a 2-person intravenous safety pilot. FDA's 2026 briefing found "insufficient clinical safety information to characterize the safety profile" of BPC-157.

**GHK-Cu human data.** These are mostly topical, with mixed results.

- In a 13-patient randomized study after CO2 laser resurfacing (Miller et al., 2006), GHK-Cu products showed no objective difference versus the regimen without GHK-Cu in redness, wrinkles, or skin quality, though patient satisfaction was higher.
- In an 86-patient randomized trial in venous leg ulcers (Bishop et al., 1992), a "tripeptide copper complex" cream did no better than placebo. The abstract does not name the tripeptide.
- The Mateescu review cites a 2026 split-face eyebrow study in 18 participants that reported positive cosmetic hair results without defining GHK-Cu speciation.
- A placebo-controlled phase 2 trial of a topical GHK-Cu gel for acute skin wounds (NCT07437586) is recruiting and has no results yet.

## Topical vs injectable: keep them separate

Cosmetic studies measure appearance outcomes for a specific cream or serum on skin. They do not establish what happens when GHK-Cu is injected, where exposure, copper release, and immunogenicity are different questions. FDA lists GHK-Cu "for injectable routes of administration" with the note that injectable products "may pose risk for immunogenicity due to the potential for aggregation and peptide-related impurities" and that "there are limited data in humans."

Hair claims need care too: a frequently cited follicle paper (Pyo et al., 2007) tested AHK-Cu, a different copper tripeptide, in cultured follicles, not GHK-Cu in people. BPC-157 has no comparable topical evidence line.

## Regulatory and legal status (checked October 2026)

**FDA approval.** Neither compound is an FDA-approved drug. For how approval, 503A compounding, and RUO labels differ, see [Are research peptides legal? FDA, RUO & compounding explained](/guides/peptide-legality-fda-ruo-compounding) and [FDA peptide reclassification 2026](/blog/fda-peptide-reclassification-2026-what-researchers-need-to-know).

**Compounding (BPC-157).** FDA's page on bulk substances that may present significant safety risks (content current as of 22 April 2026) lists BPC-157 in the table of substances "previously in category 2" whose nominations "were withdrawn by the nominators." FDA kept its concerns (immunogenicity, impurities, and "no, or only limited, safety-related information"). Off Category 2 is not permission to compound. FDA's 503A categories list (updated 14 May 2026) places BPC-157 in neither Category 1 nor Category 2. For the July 23–24, 2026 Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) meeting, FDA staff reviewed BPC-157 for ulcerative colitis and proposed not adding it to the 503A Bulks List. The committee voted to recommend inclusion anyway (BMJ reporting); FDA calls such recommendations non-binding. In a 1 October 2026 Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health Q&A, Professor S. Bryn Austin said FDA "has not yet announced if it will accept or reject the committee's recommendation." On 7 October 2026 the Washington Post reported, citing unnamed sources, that the administration was crafting plans to allow the temporary sale of certain previously restricted peptides through compounding pharmacies. The plan had not been announced. As of 11 October 2026, no FDA or HHS announcement of such a plan, no proposed rule, and no final 503A listing for BPC-157 was identified.

**Compounding (GHK-Cu).** The same FDA page lists GHK-Cu (for injectable routes of administration) in that withdrawn-nomination table, with its safety concerns kept. Non-injectable GHK-Cu is handled separately: FDA's 503A categories list (updated 14 May 2026) puts "GHK-Cu (except for injectable routes of administration)" back in Category 1, "under evaluation," after one nominator said it meant to withdraw only the injectable route. Category 1 is an interim review status, not a 503A Bulks List listing and not FDA approval. FDA's PCAC page lists GHK-Cu for discussion at a meeting to be held "before the end of February 2027." A scheduled discussion is not a listing.

**Cosmetic status (GHK-Cu).** FDA says cosmetic ingredients other than color additives need no premarket approval, and intended use decides whether a product is a cosmetic or a drug. Copper tripeptide-1 in a serum does not make an injectable vial a cosmetic or an approved drug.

**RUO labels.** In an August 2026 warning letter to Peptide Partners LLC, FDA found products were intended as drugs for human use despite "for research use only" labeling.

**WADA (2026 and 2027 lists).** BPC-157 is named under S0 Non-approved substances, prohibited at all times. GHK-Cu is not named. S0 is a catch-all for substances without human therapeutic approval, so athletes should ask their anti-doping organization. The 2027 List (effective 1 January 2027) adds "peptides" to S0's examples of non-approved substances; BPC-157 remains named and GHK-Cu still is not.

## What a CoA can and can't tell you

A certificate of analysis can show whether the submitted sample's mass matches the label (average molecular weight about 1419.5 g/mol for BPC-157 free base) and what HPLC purity it had under that lab's method. For GHK-Cu, a useful CoA would also report copper content. The Mateescu review notes that copper occupancy and free copper are usually not resolved in delivery studies.

It cannot show that other lots match, that a vial is sterile or endotoxin-free unless tested, or that the compound is safe, effective, or legal for any use. ## FAQ

### Are BPC-157 and GHK-Cu related?

No. BPC-157 is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide. GHK-Cu is the copper complex of the naturally occurring tripeptide Gly-His-Lys. They have different sequences, sizes, and research histories.

### How does the human evidence differ?

They have different kinds of human evidence. GHK-Cu has small topical cosmetic studies with mixed results. BPC-157 has a few small uncontrolled pilot reports. Neither has controlled trials establishing efficacy for injected use.

### Do GHK-Cu skin cream studies apply to injected GHK-Cu?

No. Topical studies test a specific formulation on skin. They do not establish safety or effects for injection, and FDA notes limited human data for injectable GHK-Cu.

### Is BPC-157 still on FDA's Category 2 list?

FDA's page now lists BPC-157 among nominations withdrawn from Category 2, with its safety concerns kept. A July 2026 advisory committee recommended it for the 503A Bulks List, but as of October 2026 no final FDA listing was identified.

### Is GHK-Cu legal because it is in cosmetics?

Cosmetic ingredients do not need FDA premarket approval, but intended use decides whether a product is a cosmetic or a drug. A cosmetic serum's status does not make an injectable vial an approved drug.

### Are they banned in sport?

BPC-157 is named under S0 on the WADA 2026 Prohibited List and remains named on the 2027 List. GHK-Cu is not named on either, but S0 covers unapproved substances generally, and the 2027 List adds peptides to its examples, so athletes should check with their anti-doping organization.
