## What a peptide is

A **peptide** is a short chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Proteins are longer amino-acid chains; peptides are typically shorter. The body makes many peptides as signaling molecules. Labs can also synthesize sequences for experiments—copies of natural peptides or designed analogs.

Useful starter vocabulary:

- **Identity / sequence** — which amino acids, in which order
- **Purity** — how much of the sample is the intended peptide (often reported by HPLC)
- **Identity confirmation** — often mass spectrometry (MS) matching expected molecular weight
- **Lyophilized** — freeze-dried powder form common in research catalogs

“Research peptide” is **not** an FDA quality grade. It usually means a seller is marketing a synthetic peptide for laboratory or non-clinical investigation, often with a “research use only” disclaimer. That label is about claimed intended use—not purity, sterility, or status as a medicine.

## Research vs FDA-approved vs compounded

Keep these three lanes separate. Confusing them is the most common beginner error.

### FDA-approved peptide drugs

Some peptides are approved medicines (specific branded products, manufacturers, indications, and labeling reviewed by FDA). Approval applies to a **finished drug product**—not to every online vial that shares a similar name.

### Compounded peptides (pharmacy pathway)

Compounding is preparing a customized medication under pharmacy rules and federal compounding law (commonly discussed as **503A** patient-specific pharmacies and **503B** outsourcing facilities). What may be compounded is limited by statute and FDA lists/policies—not by online popularity.

Compounding is **not** the same as FDA approval of a new drug, and it is **not** the same as buying a research-labeled vial from a website.

### Research-use / RUO marketplace peptides

Many vendors sell lyophilized peptides labeled **“for research use only,” “not for human consumption,”** or similar. In legitimate science, RUO reagents let labs study molecules without treating them as approved drugs. In consumer markets, the same disclaimer is often a marketing posture.

A research disclaimer does **not** prove laboratory quality, and it does not create FDA approval. Sellers’ overall marketing can still be evaluated as drug “intended use.”

| Lane | Usually means | Is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FDA-approved drug | Specific product approved for labeled use | Any vial with the same peptide name |
| Compounded medication | Pharmacy-prepared Rx under compounding rules | Open-web RUO sales |
| Research / RUO listing | Marketed for lab/research context | Proof of human safety or approval |

## How to think about evidence (briefly)

1. Cell / animal studies generate hypotheses
2. Human safety / PK studies describe behavior in people
3. Controlled efficacy trials test whether something works for a defined condition
4. Regulatory approval is a formal product benefit–risk decision

Many popular research peptides sit mostly on rung 1. Preclinical interest ≠ proven therapy. When marketing asserts healing or performance outcomes without naming human trial design, treat it as a claim until you can verify primary literature.

## COA literacy

A **Certificate of Analysis (COA)** is a lab report describing tests on a batch. Peptidemap’s framing treats third-party COAs as a core quality signal and maintains tools to compare vendors and see which labs appear in documentation. A COA helps only if you can read and verify it.

### What to look for

- Compound name / identity matching the listing
- **Batch / lot number** matching the vial
- **HPLC purity** (or equivalent) with a dated method/report—not a bare “99%” slogan
- **Mass spectrometry (MS)** or other identity confirmation when available
- **Testing lab name** you can independently identify
- Analysis date meaningful for that lot
- Optional depending on work: endotoxin, residual solvents, appearance, storage notes

### Common COA traps

- Pretty PDFs with no lot number, no lab identity, or identical numbers across unrelated products
- In-house-only purity claims with no path to verify externally
- COA for a different size, salt form, blend, or compound than the listing
- Vendor-hosted images that cannot be confirmed against the lab’s own records

Peptidemap’s editorial library includes [how to verify a peptide vendor COA](/blog/how-to-verify-a-peptide-vendor-certificate-of-analysis), and the [testing-labs area](/testing-labs) groups vendors by third-party lab names appearing in documentation. Use those as a map—then still verify lot-level documents yourself.

**Hard truth:** a real lot COA does not guarantee every vial is identical, sterile, correctly stored, or appropriate for any particular experiment. It is a transparency tool, not a magic shield.

## Vendor red flags (and green flags)

### Red flags

- No COA, or COA only after purchase with no sample report
- Only internal purity claims with no identifiable third party
- Therapeutic claims or outcome marketing next to an RUO disclaimer
- No verifiable business identity
- Pressure tactics or “miracle” language
- Refusal to answer basic identity/purity documentation questions
- Undocumented “too good to be true” offers (this is not a price guide—undocumented cheap is the risk signal)

### Green flags (still not guarantees)

- Lot-matched third-party HPLC (± MS) available before purchase
- Clear naming (including salt/form when relevant)
- Traceable company identity and responsive documentation requests
- Appearance in directories that emphasize verification (Peptidemap compares suppliers and lab-testing signals)—**listing is not endorsement of human use**

## Common beginner mistakes

1. Treating RUO as “approved enough”
2. Skipping lot-level COA verification
3. Confusing blends, fragments, and salt forms with parent compounds
4. Equating animal papers with human outcomes
5. Ignoring sport or workplace rules that may still apply to online products
6. Assuming “research grade” has a fixed legal definition—inspect documentation instead

## FAQs

### Are research peptides the same as prescription peptide drugs?

Not necessarily. Some peptide molecules have approved drug products; many marketplace “research peptides” are unapproved substances sold with research disclaimers. A shared name is not the same as an approved finished drug.

### What does “research use only” mean?

It is a seller’s claimed intended-use framing—typically not marketed as a consumer medicine. It does not prove quality, and it does not create FDA approval. See the legality companion for how FDA looks at intended use.

### What is a COA, simply?

A Certificate of Analysis is a batch test report—ideally from an identifiable lab—covering identity/purity (and sometimes other attributes). Match lot numbers and verify when possible.

### Can Peptidemap tell me which peptide to use for a goal?

No. Tools help compare vendors and documentation. Personal treatment decisions are medical/legal questions this guide does not answer.

### Where do I check if something can be compounded?

Not on this beginner page. Use the companion legality guide and current FDA compounding sources (and educational trackers such as PrescribedRX’s status page) because interim categories and advisory votes change.

### Why do vendors differ so much?

Synthesis quality, testing practices, storage, naming honesty, and regulatory posture all vary. Documentation literacy matters more than slogans.
