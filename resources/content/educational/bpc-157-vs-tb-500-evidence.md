## What each is

### BPC-157

BPC-157 (Body Protection Compound-157) is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide (a pentadecapeptide). It was first described in the early 1990s in work associated with researchers at the University of Zagreb and is often called a “stable gastric pentadecapeptide” because it was derived from a sequence related to human gastric juice and has been studied for stability in harsh gastrointestinal environments. It is also known in the literature under development names such as PL-14736, PLD-116, Bepecin, and PCO-02.

Neither the U.S. FDA nor other major drug regulators have approved BPC-157 as a medicine. A Phase 1 oral safety and pharmacokinetics trial (NCT02637284) was registered years ago. Its ClinicalTrials.gov status is listed as unknown, and it did not lead to an approved human use.

### Thymosin beta-4 (Tβ4), the full-length protein

Thymosin beta-4 is an endogenous human protein encoded by the *TMSB4X* gene (UniProt P62328). The mature chain is 43 amino acids long, with an acetylated serine at its start, and it is present in many mammalian cells. PubChem lists it under the international nonproprietary name timbetasin (CID 16132341), with a molecular weight of about 4,963 g/mol. Researchers study it mainly for actin binding, cell migration, and wound repair.

Full-length Tβ4 is the molecule that drug developers have taken into human trials, mostly as eye drops (RGN-259) and topical gels for skin wounds. Those trials are described below. No Tβ4 product has reached FDA approval.

### TB-500, the fragment sold under that name

“TB-500” is a marketplace name. For chemical identity, this page treats the U.S. FDA Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee briefing for 23–24 July 2026 as the source of record ([FDA PCAC briefing, media/193349](https://www.fda.gov/media/193349/download)). That briefing describes TB-500 (free base) as “a seven amino acid synthetic fragment of thymosin beta-4 (β4) from amino acids 17 to 23,” with an N-terminal acetyl: Ac-Leu-Lys-Lys-Thr-Glu-Thr-Gln-OH (Ac-LKKTETQ). It also calls TB-500 “a synthetic N-acetylated heptapeptide containing the amino acid sequence 17 through 23 (LKKTETQ) of the full-length (43-amino acid) peptide thymosin β4,” and states that “thymosin beta-4 and TB-500 are not the same substance.” PubChem lists TB-500 as Ac-Leu-Lys-Lys-Thr-Glu-Thr-Gln-OH (CID 62707662), with a molecular weight of about 889 g/mol, roughly one-sixth the mass of full-length Tβ4.

Vendors sometimes blur the two labels. Some vendors use “TB-500” for the 17–23 fragment and others for full-length synthetic Tβ4, so the label alone does not show which molecule a vial contains. Neither is the trial material (such as RGN-259) tested in clinical studies. TB-500 is not an FDA-approved drug for injury recovery or any other indication.

## Mechanisms (what researchers propose)

Mechanistic language below summarizes **preclinical** (mostly rodent and cell) findings. Proposed pathways do not prove that either peptide heals injuries in people.

### BPC-157 — proposed pathways

Peer-reviewed reviews of the musculoskeletal literature describe multifactorial, “pleiotropic” signaling rather than a single receptor target. Commonly discussed themes in animal and in vitro work include:

- **Angiogenesis and growth signaling** — upregulation of VEGF / VEGFR2-related pathways and other cell-growth cascades (for example ERK1/2, Akt)
- **Nitric oxide (NO) modulation** — effects on NOS / eNOS-related pathways and vascular tone
- **Anti-inflammatory signaling** — reduced markers such as COX-2 activity or cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) in some models
- **Tendon fibroblast biology** — increased migration, survival under stress, and growth-hormone receptor expression in cultured tendon cells
- **Tissue outcomes in animals** — improved structural and functional measures after experimental muscle, tendon, ligament, and some bone injuries

A 2025 systematic review in *HSS Journal* (Vasireddi et al.) synthesized this literature for orthopaedic sports medicine and emphasized that almost all included outcome studies were preclinical.

### Thymosin β4 and TB-500 — proposed pathways

Foundational reviews of thymosin β4 (including Goldstein, Hannappel & Kleinman, *Trends in Molecular Medicine*, 2005) describe it as a major **actin-sequestering** protein: it binds G-actin and supports cell motility, which is central to wound repair and tissue remodeling. Related preclinical themes include:

- Cell migration and cytoskeletal reorganization
- Angiogenesis and matrix remodeling in wound models
- Broader repair biology across skin, cornea, and other tissues in animal work

A key distinction for readers: **full-length Tβ4 biology is not automatically evidence for the TB-500 fragment**. In FDA’s July 2026 briefing, TB-500 is a short synthetic piece of the parent molecule; vendor usage varies. A 2026 scoping review of 80 studies found that most work evaluated full-length Tβ4, and that direct TB-500 evidence was limited to a single study of metabolites and cultured fibroblasts. Mechanistic plausibility for actin-related repair does not equal proven efficacy of injectable TB-500 for human musculoskeletal injuries.

### Complementary ≠ proven combination

Online stacking narratives often say BPC-157 is more “local / growth-factor oriented” and TB-500 more “systemic / cell-migration oriented.” That framing is a mechanistic hypothesis. It is **not** supported by combination trials in humans (see the Wolverine stack section below).

## Animal vs human evidence limits

### The evidence ladder, plainly

1. **Cell and animal studies** can show biological activity and generate hypotheses.
2. **Human pharmacokinetics and safety trials** test how a compound behaves in people.
3. **Randomized, controlled efficacy trials** are what establish whether a treatment works for a defined condition.

For both marketplace peptides, especially for injectable use aimed at tendon, ligament, or muscle repair, the literature is heavily weighted toward step 1.

### BPC-157 evidence status

**Preclinical (stronger column):** Multiple animal models report faster or better healing of experimental tendon transections, muscle injuries, ligament injuries, and related endpoints. Preclinical safety papers have reported no acute organ toxicity in animals, but that does not establish human safety for chronic or clinical use.

**Human (much thinner):** The Vasireddi et al. systematic review included 36 studies, **35 preclinical and 1 clinical**. The clinical report was a small retrospective series of intra-articular BPC-157 for chronic knee pain without a control group, blinding, or standardized imaging endpoints. A 2026 systematic review in *Acta Orthopaedica Belgica* (Poolton et al.) searched to 1 June 2026 and found three human studies with 30 participants in total: a retrospective knee-injection series, a retrospective interstitial cystitis pilot, and a two-person intravenous safety pilot. It judged all three to be of poor methodological quality and rated the overall certainty of evidence very low. A narrative review (*Current Reviews in Musculoskeletal Medicine*, McGuire et al., 2025) similarly concludes that human data remain extremely limited.

**Bottom line for BPC-157:** Animal work is extensive relative to many research peptides. **No randomized controlled human trial has shown that injectable BPC-157 heals sports injuries.**

### Full-length thymosin β4: what the human trials tested

Full-length Tβ4 has a real clinical-trial record, but almost all of it is on the surface of the eye or the skin, and the results are mixed.

- **Eye (RGN-259 eye drops).** In a Phase 3 trial in neurotrophic keratopathy (SEER-1, NCT02600429; Sosne et al., *Int J Mol Sci* 2022), complete healing at four weeks occurred in 6 of 10 treated participants and 1 of 8 on placebo (p = 0.0656). The authors called this a trend; it did not reach the conventional significance threshold, and the trial ended early after 18 participants for a business decision. In dry eye, a 72-person Phase 2 trial (Sosne & Ousler, *Clin Ophthalmol* 2015) missed both primary endpoints, though some secondary endpoints favored Tβ4, and a 9-person Phase 2 trial in severe dry eye (Sosne et al., *Cornea* 2015) reported improvements in signs and symptoms. In the larger Phase 3 dry-eye trials ARISE-2 (NCT02974907, 601 enrolled) and ARISE-3 (NCT03937882, 700 enrolled), the results posted on ClinicalTrials.gov show primary-endpoint changes close to placebo. In ARISE-3, mean change in ocular discomfort at day 15 was −0.4 in both arms. A further neurotrophic keratopathy trial, SEER-2 (NCT05555589), is listed as recruiting.
- **Skin (topical Tβ4 gel, including RGN-137).** A Phase 2 dose-escalation trial in venous stasis ulcers randomized 73 patients at eight European sites (Guarnera et al., *Ann N Y Acad Sci* 2010). It reported a safety profile comparable to placebo, and its efficacy findings were exploratory. A pressure-ulcer Phase 2 trial (NCT00382174) enrolled 72 patients. Two epidermolysis bullosa trials stopped early: NCT00311766 for lack of patient availability and expiration of study drug, and the RGN-137 trial NCT03578029 after enrolling 4 participants, for a business decision.
- **Injected recombinant Tβ4.** Two Phase 2 trials after heart attack have been run in China (NCT05485818 and NCT05984134). ClinicalTrials.gov lists both as completed, with no posted results.

The 2026 scoping review by McGuire, Hughes, Maak, and Cushman (*Applied Sciences*) found human Tβ4 evidence concentrated in eye and skin settings and identified **no human interventional studies** of administered Tβ4 or TB-500 in tendon, ligament, muscle, bone, cartilage, or spine.

### TB-500 evidence status

**Preclinical:** Direct TB-500 research is thin. The scoping review above found one direct TB-500 study, on metabolites and cultured fibroblasts. A 2026 rat Achilles study used material its authors called “synthetic thymosin beta-4 (TB-500)” (see the Wolverine stack section).

**Human:** No published human study of TB-500 was identified. FDA’s July 2026 PCAC briefing states: “The nomination did not include, and FDA has not identified, any clinical studies or human exposure data using TB-500 via any ROA. Therefore, potential safety risks associated with the use of TB-500 in humans are unknown.” (ROA means route of administration.) The same briefing says that “no articles were found in which TB-500 was administered to humans.” One Phase 1/2 safety trial of “TB-500 (Thymosin Beta 4 17-23 Fragment)” in adults with stable atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (TBRIDGE-CV, NCT07487363) was first posted on ClinicalTrials.gov on 23 March 2026. It is listed as recruiting, with an estimated 80 participants and no results posted.

**Bottom line for TB-500:** Do not read Tβ4 eye-drop or skin-gel trials as evidence that injected TB-500 repairs musculoskeletal injuries in people. They tested a different, much larger molecule by a different route.

### What absence of evidence means (and does not mean)

Missing human RCTs does **not** prove these peptides “don’t work.” It also does **not** prove they do. It means anyone claiming settled human efficacy, or settled human safety, for injectable injury use is saying more than the published record supports.

## The “Wolverine stack” nickname: what was and was not studied

“Wolverine stack” is a nickname that vendors and online communities use for pairing BPC-157 with TB-500, often sold as a pre-mixed blend. A pre-mixed blend still contains two separate peptides; it is not one molecule. It is a marketing and community label. It is not a trial name, a regulatory category, or a protocol. Common justifications for the pairing are:

- Different proposed mechanisms might be complementary
- Testimonials from clinics or forums, which are not controlled evidence
- Marketing convenience (one vial instead of two)

**What is actually published about combining them?**

No human trial has studied BPC-157 and TB-500 together. We did not identify peer-reviewed human studies that prospectively test co-administration for safety, pharmacokinetics, interactions, or musculoskeletal efficacy.

One retrospective clinic chart review of knee injections (Lee & Padgett, *Altern Ther Health Med* 2021; PMID 34324435) included 4 patients who received BPC-157 with thymosin β4. It had no control group and did not identify the thymosin β4 material, so it is not a trial of the pair.

One animal study is on the record. Biçer and colleagues compared BPC-157, TB-500, and the combination in a rat Achilles tendon healing model (histopathology and biomechanics): Biçer O, et al. *Jt Dis Relat Surg* 2026;37(3):822–837 ([full text](https://www.jointdrs.org/full-text/1851); DOI 10.52312/jdrs.2026.2951; PMID 42542926). The abstract states: “Combined BPC-157 and TB-500 treatment did not confer additional benefits compared to either agent alone.” Its results paragraph covers maximum load to failure, total Bonar score, and total Movin score. The discussion states that the combination “did not demonstrate superior biomechanical or histopathological outcomes compared to monotherapy.”

This is a rat study, not a human trial. It is not a protocol, and it is not a reason to combine the two. The paper calls its material “synthetic thymosin beta-4 (TB-500).” It was **not** confirmed that the vial was the 7-residue Ac-LKKTETQ fragment rather than full-length thymosin β4, so do not read the rat result as a test of the FDA briefing’s heptapeptide specifically. Blend calculators and timing schedules circulating online are not derived from clinical trials of the pair.

A July 2026 advisory committee vote does not change this picture. The PCAC voted on each substance separately, not on the pair, and a committee vote is not an FDA approval (see below).

**Educational takeaway:** Treat “Wolverine stack” claims as **untested hypotheses**, not as evidence-based regimens. This page intentionally does not provide combination ratios, schedules, or administration guidance.

## FDA / sport context

Regulatory status changes; verify current primary sources before relying on any summary, including this one.

### FDA: not approved drugs; compounding is a separate question

- **Neither BPC-157 nor TB-500 is an FDA-approved drug** for any indication.
- FDA’s page “Certain Bulk Drug Substances for Use in Compounding that May Present Significant Safety Risks” (content current as of 22 April 2026) lists BPC-157 and “Thymosin beta-4, fragment (LKKTETQ), also known as TB-500” in its table “Bulk drug substances nominated but withdrawn”, which FDA describes as substances previously in category 2 of its interim policies that the nominators withdrew. For BPC-157 it describes immunogenicity risk for some routes, peptide impurity and API characterization complexity, and “no, or only limited, safety-related information for the proposed routes of administration.” For TB-500 it states, in FDA’s wording: “FDA has not identified any human exposure data drug products containing Thymosin Beta-4, Fragment. FDA lacks important information regarding any safety issues raised by this drug, including whether it would cause harm if administered to humans.”
- For the 23–24 July 2026 Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) meeting, FDA staff reviewed TB-500 for the **section 503A Bulks List** for the nominated use of wound healing and concluded: “we propose not adding TB-500 (free base) or TB-500 acetate to the 503A Bulks List.”
- The committee voted the other way. The *BMJ* reported on 28 July 2026 that PCAC recommended six peptides, including BPC-157 and TB-500, each by a narrow majority; the one peptide it did not recommend was emideltide (DSIP). FDA staff had proposed against BPC-157 as well: its separate briefing concluded, “we propose not adding BPC-157 (free base) or BPC-157 acetate to the 503A Bulks List” ([FDA PCAC briefing, media/193343](https://www.fda.gov/media/193343/download), PDF p. 47). **PCAC votes are advisory and non-binding.** FDA’s briefing states that it “will not issue a final determination on the issues at hand until input from the advisory committee process has been considered and all reviews have been finalized.” A favorable vote is not FDA final listing, not marketing approval, and not lawful compounding authority by itself.

“Research use only” labeling on online vials does not create a legal pathway for human treatment and does not substitute for pharmaceutical quality systems. For pathway literacy (Category 2 removal is not Category 1 and is not the 503A Bulks List), see [Are research peptides legal? FDA, RUO, and compounding](/guides/peptide-legality-fda-ruo-compounding).

### Department of Defense / service members

The DoD Operation Supplement Safety (OPSS) program describes BPC-157 as an **unapproved drug** and lists it among prohibited dietary supplement ingredients for service members. OPSS notes that marketed benefits have been shown mainly in lab or animal studies and that well-designed human evidence for safety and effectiveness is lacking.

### Sport / WADA

Under the **WADA 2026 Prohibited List** (effective 1 January 2026):

- **BPC-157** is named under **S0 Non-approved substances** (prohibited at all times).
- **Thymosin-β4 and its derivatives, e.g. TB-500**, are named under **S2.3 Growth factors and growth factor modulators** (prohibited at all times). That entry covers full-length Tβ4 as well as the fragment.

Athletes in tested sports should treat products containing either peptide, including blends, as a doping risk regardless of intent or “research chemical” labeling. Individual leagues may have additional policies.

## FAQs

### Are BPC-157 and TB-500 the same peptide?

No. BPC-157 is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide from the gastric-cytoprotection research line. TB-500 usually refers to a short synthetic fragment of thymosin beta-4, a different endogenous protein involved in actin binding and cell migration. They are chemically unrelated.

### Is TB-500 the same as thymosin beta-4?

No. Full-length thymosin beta-4 is a 43-amino-acid human protein. In FDA’s July 2026 briefing, TB-500 is a 7-amino-acid acetylated fragment, Ac-LKKTETQ, which matches residues 17 to 23 of thymosin beta-4; vendors do not always use the name this way. The same briefing states that “thymosin beta-4 and TB-500 are not the same substance.” Full-length thymosin beta-4 has been tested in human trials as eye drops and skin gels, with mixed results. No published human study of TB-500 was identified.

### Is either one FDA-approved for healing injuries?

No. Neither is an FDA-approved drug for musculoskeletal healing or any other indication. Compounding-list discussions, including PCAC recommendations, are not the same as drug approval.

### Does animal evidence mean they work in humans?

No. Animal and cell studies can be scientifically interesting and still fail to predict human outcomes. For injectable injury recovery claims, neither peptide has randomized controlled human trial evidence.

### What is the “Wolverine stack”?

It is a nickname that vendors and online communities use for pairing BPC-157 with TB-500. It is not a trial name or a protocol. No human trial has studied the pair. A 2026 rat Achilles study (Biçer et al., *Jt Dis Relat Surg*) reported that combined BPC-157 and TB-500 did not confer additional benefits compared with either agent alone, and that paper did not confirm its “TB-500” was the Ac-LKKTETQ fragment. A July 2026 FDA advisory committee vote recommending each substance for the 503A bulks list is advisory and is not FDA approval. Peptidemap does not give dosing or combination instructions.

### Are these substances banned in sport?

Yes under WADA rules as of the 2026 Prohibited List: BPC-157 is named in S0; thymosin-β4 and derivatives including TB-500 are named in S2.3. Both classes are prohibited at all times. Check your sport’s governing body as well.

### Why do so many sites recommend them together?

They are marketed together, share overlapping online recovery audiences, and have complementary-sounding proposed mechanisms. Marketing frequency is not the same as clinical validation.
