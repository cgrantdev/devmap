## What each is

### BPC-157

BPC-157 (Body Protection Compound-157) is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide (a pentadecapeptide). It was first described in the early 1990s in work associated with researchers at the University of Zagreb and is often called a “stable gastric pentadecapeptide” because it was derived from a sequence related to human gastric juice and has been studied for stability in harsh gastrointestinal environments. It is also known in the literature under development names such as PL-14736, PLD-116, Bepecin, and PCO-02.

Neither the U.S. FDA nor other major drug regulators have approved BPC-157 as a medicine. A Phase I oral safety/pharmacokinetics trial (NCT02637284) was registered years ago; it did not lead to an approved human therapeutic use.

### TB-500

“TB-500” is a marketplace name. For chemical identity, this page treats the U.S. FDA Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee briefing for 23–24 July 2026 as the source of record ([FDA PCAC briefing, media/193349](https://www.fda.gov/media/193349/download)). That briefing describes TB-500 (free base) as “a seven amino acid synthetic fragment of thymosin beta-4 (β4) from amino acids 17 to 23,” with an N-terminal acetyl: Ac-Leu-Lys-Lys-Thr-Glu-Thr-Gln-OH (Ac-LKKTETQ). It also calls TB-500 “a synthetic N-acetylated heptapeptide containing the amino acid sequence 17 through 23 (LKKTETQ) of the full-length (43-amino acid) peptide thymosin β4,” and states that “thymosin beta-4 and TB-500 are not the same substance.” Full-length thymosin beta-4 is the endogenous 43-amino-acid peptide, present in many mammalian cells, and has been studied for wound healing, cell migration, and actin regulation. It is not TB-500. Vendors sometimes blur the label, so a vial sold as “TB-500” may not match the acetylated 17–23 fragment in the FDA briefing, and it may not match full-length Tβ4 used in pharmaceutical programs.

TB-500 (the fragment) is not an FDA-approved drug for injury recovery or any other indication. Human clinical programs that do exist in this family have largely studied topical full-length Tβ4 (for example, ophthalmic formulations), which is a different product, route, and use case from injectable TB-500 marketed for tendons or muscle.

| | BPC-157 | TB-500 (typical market meaning) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chemical identity | Synthetic 15-aa pentadecapeptide | N-acetylated heptapeptide Ac-LKKTETQ (Tβ4 residues 17–23) per the July 2026 FDA PCAC briefing; not the 43-aa protein |
| Main research tradition | Gastric cytoprotection → musculoskeletal & GI animal models | Actin regulation / cell migration → wound & tissue-repair biology |
| Human evidence for injectable MSK use | Very limited (small uncontrolled reports; no robust RCTs) | Essentially none identified for the injected fragment in tendon/muscle/ligament trials |
| FDA-approved drug? | No | No |
| WADA (2026 list) | Named under S0 (non-approved substances) | Named under S2.3 (thymosin-β4 and derivatives, e.g. TB-500) |

## Mechanisms (what researchers propose)

Mechanistic language below summarizes **preclinical** (mostly rodent and cell) findings. Proposed pathways do not prove that either peptide heals injuries in people.

### BPC-157 — proposed pathways

Peer-reviewed reviews of the musculoskeletal literature describe multifactorial, “pleiotropic” signaling rather than a single receptor target. Commonly discussed themes in animal and in vitro work include:

- **Angiogenesis and growth signaling** — upregulation of VEGF / VEGFR2-related pathways and other cell-growth cascades (for example ERK1/2, Akt)
- **Nitric oxide (NO) modulation** — effects on NOS / eNOS-related pathways and vascular tone
- **Anti-inflammatory signaling** — reduced markers such as COX-2 activity or cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) in some models
- **Tendon fibroblast biology** — increased migration, survival under stress, and growth-hormone receptor expression in cultured tendon cells
- **Tissue outcomes in animals** — improved structural and functional measures after experimental muscle, tendon, ligament, and some bone injuries

A 2025 systematic review in *HSS Journal* (Vasireddi et al.) synthesized this literature for orthopaedic sports medicine and emphasized that almost all included outcome studies were preclinical.

### TB-500 / thymosin β4 — proposed pathways

Foundational reviews of thymosin β4 (including Goldstein, Hannappel & Kleinman, *Trends in Molecular Medicine*, 2005) describe it as a major **actin-sequestering** protein: it binds G-actin and supports cell motility, which is central to wound repair and tissue remodeling. Related preclinical themes include:

- Cell migration and cytoskeletal reorganization
- Angiogenesis and matrix remodeling in wound models
- Broader repair biology across skin, cornea, and other tissues in animal work

A key distinction for readers: **full-length Tβ4 biology is not automatically evidence for the TB-500 fragment**. Marketplace TB-500 is usually a short synthetic piece of the parent molecule. Mechanistic plausibility for actin-related repair does not equal proven efficacy of injectable TB-500 for human musculoskeletal injuries.

### Complementary ≠ proven combination

Online stacking narratives often say BPC-157 is more “local / growth-factor oriented” and TB-500 more “systemic / cell-migration oriented.” That framing is a mechanistic hypothesis. It is **not** supported by combination trials in humans (see Stacking claims below).

## Animal vs human evidence limits

### The evidence ladder, plainly

1. **Cell and animal studies** can show biological activity and generate hypotheses.
2. **Human pharmacokinetics and safety trials** test how a compound behaves in people.
3. **Randomized, controlled efficacy trials** are what establish whether a treatment works for a defined condition.

For both peptides—especially for injectable use aimed at tendon, ligament, or muscle repair—the literature is heavily weighted toward step 1.

### BPC-157 evidence status

**Preclinical (stronger column):** Multiple animal models report faster or better healing of experimental tendon transections, muscle injuries, ligament injuries, and related endpoints. Preclinical safety papers have reported no acute organ toxicity at studied doses in animals, but that does not establish human safety for chronic or clinical use.

**Human (much thinner):** The Vasireddi et al. systematic review screened hundreds of publications and included 36 studies meeting criteria—**35 preclinical and 1 clinical**. The clinical report was a small retrospective series of intra-articular BPC-157 for chronic knee pain without a control group, blinding, or standardized imaging endpoints. A later narrative review (*Current Reviews in Musculoskeletal Medicine*, McGuire et al., 2025) similarly concludes that human data remain extremely limited and that BPC-157 should be treated as investigational. Small additional pilot reports exist in other settings (for example interstitial cystitis injections; a tiny IV safety pilot), but these do not constitute confirmatory musculoskeletal efficacy trials.

**Bottom line for BPC-157:** Animal work is extensive relative to many research peptides; **high-quality human evidence that injectable BPC-157 heals sports injuries is lacking**.

### TB-500 / thymosin β4 evidence status

**Preclinical:** Thymosin β4 has a substantial basic-science and animal literature on wound healing and cell migration. Musculoskeletal-specific interventional data for the **TB-500 fragment** are thinner than popular marketing implies.

**Human:** Credible late-stage human programs have focused largely on **topical full-length Tβ4** in ocular/dermal contexts—not on injectable TB-500 for Achilles, rotator cuff, or muscle repair. A 2026 scoping review on thymosin β4 and TB-500 in tissue healing (McGuire, Hughes, Maak, Cushman; *Applied Sciences*) mapped a large preclinical literature and found **no human interventional studies** of Tβ4 or TB-500 directly addressing tendon, ligament, muscle, bone, or cartilage repair in the way marketplace injury claims suggest.

**Bottom line for TB-500:** Do not equate “thymosin β4 eye-drop trials” with evidence that injected TB-500 repairs musculoskeletal injuries in people.

### What absence of evidence means (and does not mean)

Missing human RCTs does **not** prove these peptides “don’t work.” It also does **not** prove they do. It means anyone claiming settled human efficacy—or settled human safety—for injectable injury use is saying more than the published record supports.

## Stacking claims

BPC-157 and TB-500 are often sold as a pre-mixed blend or recommended together online (sometimes nicknamed a “Wolverine stack”). Common justifications are:

- Different proposed mechanisms might be complementary
- Anecdotal reports from clinics or forums
- Marketing convenience (one vial / one protocol)

**What is actually published about combining them?**

A peer-reviewed rat study is now on the record. Biçer and colleagues compared BPC-157, TB-500, and the combination in an Achilles tendon healing model (histopathology and biomechanics): Biçer O, et al. *Jt Dis Relat Surg* 2026;37(3):822–837 ([full text](https://www.jointdrs.org/full-text/1851); DOI 10.52312/jdrs.2026.2951; PMID 42542926). The abstract states: “Combined BPC-157 and TB-500 treatment did not confer additional benefits compared to either agent alone.” On the outcomes named in that results paragraph—maximum load to failure, total Bonar score, and total Movin score—the combination did not add benefit versus either agent alone. The discussion states that the combination “did not demonstrate superior biomechanical or histopathological outcomes compared to monotherapy.”

This is a rat histopathology and biomechanics study, not a human trial. It is not a protocol, and it is not a reason to stack. We still did **not** identify peer-reviewed human studies that prospectively test co-administration of BPC-157 and TB-500 for safety, pharmacokinetics, interactions, or musculoskeletal efficacy. Mechanistic complementarity in people remains speculation. Blend calculators and timing schedules circulating online are not derived from clinical trials of the pair.

The Achilles paper calls the TB-500 material “synthetic thymosin beta-4 (TB-500).” It was **not** verified that the vial was the 7-residue Ac-LKKTETQ fragment rather than full-length thymosin β4. Do not read the rat result as a test of the FDA briefing’s heptapeptide specifically.

One small retrospective clinic series of knee injections included some patients who received BPC-157 with thymosin β4; that kind of uncontrolled observation cannot validate a stack protocol.

**Educational takeaway:** Treat human stacking claims as **untested hypotheses**, not as evidence-based regimens. This page intentionally does not provide combination ratios, schedules, or administration guidance.

## FDA / sport context

Regulatory status changes; verify current primary sources before relying on any summary—including this one.

### FDA: not approved drugs; compounding is a separate question

- **Neither BPC-157 nor TB-500 is an FDA-approved drug** for any indication.
- FDA has published safety-risk commentary on peptide bulk substances used in compounding. On its “Certain Bulk Drug Substances… That May Present Significant Safety Risks” page, FDA has described concerns for **BPC-157** (immunogenicity risk for some routes; peptide impurities / API characterization complexity; limited human safety information for proposed routes) and for **thymosin beta-4 fragment (LKKTETQ), also known as TB-500** (similar impurity/immunogenicity concerns; **no identified human exposure data** for the fragment in FDA’s summary).
- In July 2026, FDA’s Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) considered nominated peptide bulk substances for the **section 503A Bulks List**. Press and legal analyses report that PCAC voted to **recommend** inclusion of several peptides including BPC-157 and TB-500. **PCAC votes are advisory and non-binding.** Favorable recommendations are not the same as FDA final listing, marketing approval, or automatic lawful compounding authority. Final status requires FDA action (typically notice-and-comment rulemaking). Readers should check current FDA compounding guidance rather than treat a committee vote as permission.

“Research use only” labeling on online vials does not create a legal pathway for human treatment and does not substitute for pharmaceutical quality systems. For pathway literacy (Category 2 removal is not Category 1 and is not the 503A Bulks List), see [Are research peptides legal? FDA, RUO, and compounding](/guides/peptide-legality-fda-ruo-compounding).

### Department of Defense / service members

The DoD Operation Supplement Safety (OPSS) program describes BPC-157 as an **unapproved drug** and lists it among prohibited dietary supplement ingredients for service members. OPSS notes that marketed benefits have been shown mainly in lab or animal studies and that well-designed human evidence for safety and effectiveness is lacking.

### Sport / WADA

Under the **WADA 2026 Prohibited List** (effective 1 January 2026):

- **BPC-157** is named under **S0 Non-approved substances** (prohibited at all times).
- **Thymosin-β4 and its derivatives, e.g. TB-500**, are named under **S2.3 Growth factors and growth factor modulators** (prohibited at all times).

Athletes in tested sports should treat products containing either peptide—including blends—as a doping risk regardless of intent or “research chemical” labeling. Individual leagues may have additional policies.

## FAQs

### Are BPC-157 and TB-500 the same peptide?

No. BPC-157 is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide from the gastric-cytoprotection research line. TB-500 usually refers to a short synthetic fragment of thymosin beta-4, a different endogenous protein involved in actin binding and cell migration. They are chemically unrelated.

### Is either one FDA-approved for healing injuries?

No. Neither is an FDA-approved drug for musculoskeletal healing or any other indication. Compounding-list discussions (including PCAC recommendations) are not the same as drug approval.

### Does animal evidence mean they work in humans?

No. Animal and cell studies can be scientifically interesting and still fail to predict human outcomes. For injectable injury recovery claims, both peptides still lack robust randomized human efficacy evidence.

### Is there research on stacking BPC-157 with TB-500?

A 2026 rat Achilles study (Biçer et al., *Jt Dis Relat Surg*) reported that combined BPC-157 and TB-500 did not confer additional benefit compared with either agent alone on maximum load to failure, total Bonar score, and total Movin score. That result is animal histopathology and biomechanics, not evidence for a human stack. We did not identify published human combination trials establishing safety or efficacy of the pair. Stacking narratives remain mostly mechanistic reasoning and anecdote.

### Are these substances banned in sport?

Yes under WADA rules as of the 2026 Prohibited List: BPC-157 is named in S0; thymosin-β4 and derivatives including TB-500 are named in S2.3. Both classes are prohibited at all times. Check your sport’s governing body as well.

### Why do so many sites recommend them together?

They are marketed together, share overlapping online recovery audiences, and have complementary-sounding proposed mechanisms. Marketing frequency is not the same as clinical validation.
