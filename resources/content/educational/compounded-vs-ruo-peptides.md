It is not medical, legal or purchasing advice, and it does not say that any compounded or RUO product is safe, effective or legal to use.*

On 28 Oct 2026 the HHS Office of the Secretary announced a draft "Enforcement Discretion Policy Regarding 503A Compounding Using Certain Peptide Bulk Drug Substances" [1][2] [primary]. It names six peptides: BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500 [1] [primary]. The Federal Register notice (FR Doc. 2026-22192, docket HHS-OASH-2026-0496, RIN 0910-ZD94) was filed on 28 Oct and published on 30 Oct 2026 at 91 FR 68526 [2] [primary]. This guide covers who can compound, what the draft does and does not do, and how compounded drugs differ from RUO-labelled peptides.

## Who can compound: 503A vs 503B

Compounding is described in the notice as "a practice in which a licensed pharmacist, a licensed physician or, in the case of an outsourcing facility, a person under the supervision of a licensed pharmacist, combines, mixes or alters ingredients of a drug to create a medication tailored to the needs of an individual patient" [2] [primary].

Federal law has two compounding tracks:

- **Section 503A** covers drugs compounded "by a licensed pharmacist in a State-licensed pharmacy or Federal facility, or by a licensed physician" [1] [primary]. If certain conditions are met, those drugs are exempt from FDA pre-market approval, the "adequate directions for use" labelling rule, and federal CGMP requirements [1] [primary]. One condition is that each drug must be compounded "based on the receipt of a valid patient specific prescription" [1] [primary]. Another condition limits which bulk ingredients can be used. The ingredient must have a USP or NF monograph, or be a component of an FDA-approved drug, or appear on the 503A Bulks List, which is set through rulemaking [1] [primary].
- **Section 503B** covers outsourcing facilities. These are the larger facilities that NPR describes as "producing higher volumes of medicines" [5] [press].

The draft is written for 503A. It names "a State-licensed pharmacy, Federal facility, or licensed physician" as the entities it covers [1] [primary]. Outsourcing facilities are not among the entities it names. NPR reports that the policy "doesn't apply to the larger ones, called 503Bs" [5] [press]. NCPA describes the policy as covering 503A compounding pharmacies [6] [press].

## What enforcement discretion does and does NOT mean

The core sentence of the draft says that, in the listed circumstances, "HHS does not intend to take enforcement action against a State-licensed pharmacy, Federal facility, or licensed physician compounding a drug product using a bulk drug substance in Appendix A" [1] [primary].

The documents say this is **not**:

- **Approval.** "Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, which means FDA does not review their safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed" [1][2] [primary]. HHS cites "the lack of availability of approved products" [2] [primary].
- **A Bulks List listing.** The draft applies to substances that are not "on the 503A bulks list codified at 21 CFR 216.23(a)" [1] [primary]. It states that "FDA's future rulemakings may propose adding some or all of these peptides" [2] [primary]. NCPA reports that pharmacies "are still awaiting final rulemaking" [6] [press].
- **Category 1.** Appendix B says each of the six was in FDA's interim Category 2 from 2023 "until April 22, 2026" [1] [primary]. None is in Category 1. The HHS draft is a separate policy.
- **Binding or permanent.** The notice says the draft "does not establish any rights for any person and is not binding on HHS or the public" [2] [primary]. It gives no end date. It applies "until a peptide bulk drug substance is addressed in a final rule as being included or not included on the 503A bulks list" [1] [primary]. NCPA notes that "no timeframe was given" [6] [press].
- **Irrevocable.** "HHS may revisit application of this interim policy (in whole or in part) if, among other things, a safety signal or product quality issue arises" [1][2] [primary].
- **A safety or efficacy finding.** The documents contain no determination that any of the six is safe or effective. Appendix B records that for each peptide "CDER recommended against listing" and that the PCAC, which met 23–24 Jul 2026 [4], voted in favour [1] [primary]. The draft was reviewed by OIRA from 14 Oct to 27 Oct 2026 under the title "Interim Access Policy for Compounding Using Certain Peptides" [3] [primary]. NPR reports that FDA's own scientists recommended against unwinding the restrictions [5] [press].

The comment window is "2 weeks after date of publication in the Federal Register," through regulations.gov on docket HHS-OASH-2026-0496 [2] [primary]. Regulations.gov lists the comment period as closing on 13 Nov 2026 at 11:59 pm Eastern Time [10] [primary].

## Scope: forms, listed routes and single ingredient

The coverage is narrow. Appendix B defines each covered identity by amino acid sequence, formula and molecular weight [1] [primary]. The forms are BPC-157 acetate, TB-500 acetate, epitalon acetate, KPV acetate, MOTS-c acetate and semax free base. Other salt forms are not listed.

The draft also lists routes. The list below describes the policy's scope only. It is not usage information.

- BPC-157 acetate: subcutaneous, intramuscular, oral and topical dosage forms
- TB-500 acetate: subcutaneous or intramuscular injectable dosage forms only
- MOTS-c acetate: subcutaneous or intramuscular injectable dosage forms
- Epitalon acetate: subcutaneous or intramuscular injectable dosage forms "dispensed in intermittent courses"
- Semax free base: intranasal use
- KPV acetate: topical and oral dosage forms [1] [primary]

The product must contain "only a single Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) listed in Appendix A" [1] [primary]. Blends fall outside the draft. That includes combinations of two listed peptides, such as BPC-157 with TB-500. The only drug delivery device that may be co-packaged with or contain the compounded drug is "an FDA 510(k)-cleared syringe" [1] [primary]. For any oral form, the compounder is expected to keep documentation showing "an adequate level of oral bioavailability in humans" [1] [primary].

## Identity, purity and adverse-event conditions, as stated

The draft does not use the word "certify." These are the conditions in its own words [1] [primary]:

- **Identity.** The substance "has the identity and limitations in route of administration described in Appendix B. The identity includes but is not limited to amino acid sequence, chemical structure, molecular weight, and molecular formula."
- **Characterization.** The substance "is from a facility registered under section 510 … and characterized and identified in a valid certificate of analysis (COA) that includes test results consistent with existing guidance-based criteria described by FDA." HHS says this "helps provide assurance that what a compounder receives from a supplier is what it claims to be and has been tested for identity, purity, potency, and the absence of harmful contaminants such as endotoxins, heavy metals, microbial contamination, and impurities."
- **Original manufacturer.** The original manufacturer, "not a subsequent packer, repacker, labeler, or distributor," must be registered under section 510 and comply with CGMP. Its facility location must be listed on the COA. An FDA-registered repacker or reseller must itself document "vendor validation" of the manufacturer and its COA. The COA requirements include the grade "Intended for human drug compounding."
- **Reporting.** Compounders must "report products compounded, serious adverse events, and product quality issues to FDA and to the States where the products are compounded and sent (if different)." Serious adverse events must be reported "regardless of whether or not considered drug related," and product-quality issues as well, to FDA's MedWatch program. Product reports must include the active ingredient and its strength, the original manufacturer with its facility identifier, and the dosage form and route. The reporting procedure "should be detailed on the product labeling provided to the patient."
- **Label.** The label must state: "This is a compounded drug product. Not for resale."
- **Other law.** The product must comply with "all other applicable requirements of the FD&C Act," including the insanitary-conditions ban and "section 503A's … patient specific prescription requirement."

## How this differs from RUO-labelled products

RUO ("research use only") products are sold with a label saying they are not for human use. The draft's conditions describe a different channel:

- **Labelling.** Covered compounded products carry the statement "This is a compounded drug product. Not for resale" [1] [primary]. RUO products carry research-only labelling.
- **Intended use.** The draft covers drug products compounded for an individual patient under section 503A [1] [primary]. RUO labelling states that a product is not intended for human use.
- **Prescription.** The draft requires a valid patient-specific prescription [1] [primary]. No prescription is involved in RUO sales.
- **Identity and purity oversight.** The draft ties coverage to a 510-registered, CGMP-compliant original manufacturer, a COA meeting FDA-referenced testing criteria, and MedWatch reporting [1] [primary]. RUO products are outside these terms, and none of the draft's identity, purity or reporting conditions apply to them.

HHS's stated rationale refers to RUO directly. The Secretary "has determined that this limited enforcement-discretion policy will discourage the continued, widespread use of certain research-use-only peptides supplied by foreign manufacturers that pose a safety risk to US patients" [1] [primary]. That is HHS's framing. The draft does not cover, authorise or legitimise RUO sales.

## State-law note

The draft mentions States in its reporting condition and covers "State-licensed" pharmacies [1] [primary]. It contains no express statement about state pharmacy law. The law firm Frier Levitt writes that the policy "does not displace independent state pharmacy board requirements" and that pharmacies must still assess state law [7] [press]. That is the firm's analysis, not text from the policy. AJMC reports that Jeff Cohen, cofounder of the American Peptide Association, told Reuters that states may not follow the federal government in relaxing rules [11] [press]. That is a reported comment, not a statement of any state's position.

## Petition and next PCAC review

On 23 Oct 2026 the Partnership for Safe Medicines (PSM) filed a citizen petition with FDA (docket FDA-2026-P-13366) [12] [primary, petitioner's filing]. It asks FDA to "refrain from taking action … to allow for compounding of drug products using any of the seven peptide bulk drug substances" considered at the 23–24 Jul 2026 PCAC meeting, and to "refrain from relying on" that meeting, which PSM says was constituted in violation of federal advisory-committee and ethics law [12]. PSM's press release describes this as urging FDA "to disregard the advisory committee vote that recommended allowing them" [13] [press release]. It is a petition to the agency, not a lawsuit, and no FDA response is reported in these sources.

AJMC reports that, as of July, the PCAC was scheduled to review five more unapproved peptides for pharmacy compounding in February [11] [press]. FDA's PCAC page says FDA "will host an advisory committee meeting before the end of February 2027" [14] [primary]. A scheduled review is not a listing.

## Litigation context: Eli Lilly's retatrutide suits

On 12 Aug 2026, Eli Lilly announced six US lawsuits over unapproved retatrutide. Retatrutide is an investigational drug that Lilly says is in Phase 3 trials and not approved anywhere [8] [company-reported]. Lilly says the suits target "compounding pharmacies, medical spas, and online sellers that falsely claim their drugs are for 'research-use only' when they are actually intended for human use" [8] [company-reported]. The six cases it lists were filed in federal district courts in California and Texas [8] [company-reported]. Reuters reported the filings the same day [9] [press]. These are allegations, and no ruling is reported in these sources. Retatrutide is not one of the six peptides in the HHS draft.

## FAQ

### Did FDA approve these six peptides?

No. The notice comes from the HHS Office of the Secretary. It says compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, and none of the six is on the 503A Bulks List [1][2] [primary].

### Is the policy final?

No. The Federal Register notice calls it a draft, says it is not binding, and opens a two-week comment window after publication on 30 Oct 2026 [2] [primary].

### How long does it last?

No end date is given. It applies until a final rule addresses each substance, and HHS may revisit it at any time [1] [primary].

### Does it cover blends such as BPC-157 with TB-500?

No. The draft requires a single listed active ingredient [1] [primary].

### Does it cover RUO-labelled products?

No. Its terms depend on 503A compounding, a patient-specific prescription and registered CGMP sourcing. HHS calls foreign RUO peptides a safety risk [1] [primary].

### Did FDA scientists support it?

Appendix B records that CDER recommended against listing each of the six and that the PCAC voted in favour [1] [primary].
