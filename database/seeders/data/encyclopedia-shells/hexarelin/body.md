# Hexarelin

## Field: overviewShort

**Hexarelin** (INN **examorelin**; development codes EP-23905 and MF-6003) is a synthetic **growth hormone-releasing peptide (GHRP)**. It is a six-residue analog of GHRP-6 that acts on the ghrelin receptor (GHS-R1a) and also binds the scavenger receptor **CD36** in heart tissue. It was studied in small human physiology studies published between 1994 and 2004. It is **not approved** by the FDA or any other regulator found in the sources checked, and the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) prohibits it at all times. Educational only. Not medical advice.

## Field: overview

Hexarelin was first described in 1994 as an analog of GHRP-6 in which the tryptophan at position 2 is replaced by the chemically more stable 2-methyl-tryptophan (Life Sci 1994). In healthy volunteers it produced strong, dose-dependent growth hormone (GH) release after intravenous, subcutaneous, intranasal, and oral administration, with much lower biological bioavailability (estimated from the GH response) by the nasal and oral routes (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1994). It is not selective for GH: human studies also recorded rises in prolactin, ACTH, and cortisol. Repeated administration over weeks to months produced a partial, reversible fall in the GH response. A second line of research, mostly in animals plus a few single-administration human studies, examined effects on the heart that appear not to depend on GH. In 2002 researchers identified CD36 as a cardiac binding site for hexarelin. No marketing authorization was found in Drugs@FDA or in the EMA medicines database, and ClinicalTrials.gov lists no registered studies under either name. **It is not an approved medicine.** Peptidemap is a research-use comparison publisher, not a clinic, pharmacy, or supplier. This page makes no supply, availability, vendor, or price claims about hexarelin.

## Field: background

The FDA Global Substance Registration System (GSRS) lists the substance under its INN **examorelin**, with UNII **09QF37C617**, CAS Registry Number **140703-51-1**, and the synonyms hexarelin, EP-23905, and MF-6003. GSRS and PubChem (CID 6918297) give the systematic name L-histidyl-2-methyl-D-tryptophyl-L-alanyl-L-tryptophyl-D-phenylalanyl-L-lysinamide and the molecular formula C47H58N12O6. The peptide differs from GHRP-6 (His-D-Trp-Ala-Trp-D-Phe-Lys-NH2) only by a methyl group on the indole ring of the second residue. The first report came from the Department of Pharmacology at the University of Milan (Life Sci 1994). Much of the early human work came from the University of Turin, and the 1994 double-blind dose-response study in volunteers was led by authors from the medical department of Mediolanum Farmaceutici (Milan). R. Deghenghi, a co-author on many of these papers, later reviewed the program from Europeptides (Argenteuil, France) and noted that oral absorption of these peptides rarely exceeded 1% (Acta Paediatr Suppl 1997). A naming caution: WADA lists **alexamorelin** separately from examorelin (hexarelin), and rat heart studies used **EP 51389** as a comparator GH-releasing peptide. These are different molecules, and their data do not transfer to hexarelin. Product labels such as "hexarelin acetate" name a salt form. PubChem depositor records and chemical catalogs list the acetate under CAS 208251-52-9. The identity data on this page refer to the peptide as registered in GSRS and PubChem.

## Field: mechanismOfActionIntro

Hexarelin belongs to the peptidyl GH secretagogue family with GHRP-6, GHRP-2 (pralmorelin), and ipamorelin. These peptides act through the GH secretagogue receptor type 1a (GHS-R1a), which was later shown to be the receptor for the stomach hormone ghrelin. This is a different receptor from the one used by GHRH analogs such as sermorelin, tesamorelin, and CJC-1295. Unlike the non-peptide MK-0677, hexarelin also binds CD36 in heart tissue. In the same binding assay, MK-0677 and the peptide EP 51389 did not displace it from that site.

## Field: mechanismSubsections

### subsection 1 — title: GHS-R1a and GH release
**items:**
- item: Shared pituitary binding site — description: In 1996 researchers identified a high-affinity, G-protein-linked binding site in pituitary membranes shared by MK-0677, L-692,429, GHRP-6, and hexarelin. Binding affinity tracked GH-releasing activity, and GHRH and somatostatin did not displace it (Mol Endocrinol 1996).
- item: Synergy with GHRH — description: A low hexarelin dose given with GHRH produced a large GH release with a moderate prolactin rise and no cortisol rise (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1996).
- item: Relation to ghrelin — description: After ghrelin was identified as the natural ligand of GHS-R1a, a comparison in seven young men found that ghrelin released more GH than hexarelin at the same dose. Giving hexarelin together with ghrelin did not change the response to ghrelin (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2001).

### subsection 2 — title: Effects beyond growth hormone
**items:**
- item: Prolactin, ACTH, and cortisol — description: Hexarelin raised prolactin, ACTH, and cortisol in healthy adults. Its ACTH and cortisol effect was similar in size to that of human corticotropin-releasing hormone (Peptides 1997; J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1997).
- item: Cushing's disease — description: In patients with pituitary Cushing's disease, the ACTH rise after hexarelin was about 7-fold greater than after human corticotropin-releasing hormone. Patients whose Cushing's syndrome came from an adrenal adenoma or ectopic ACTH showed no response (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1997).
- item: Age pattern — description: The GH response rose sharply at puberty, stayed similar in young adults and was lower in elderly people; the ACTH response was also enhanced at puberty. Prolactin and cortisol responses did not vary with age (Eur J Endocrinol 1997).

### subsection 3 — title: CD36 and the heart
**items:**
- item: Cardiac binding protein — description: A photoactivatable hexarelin derivative labelled a protein of about 84 kDa in rat heart membranes, with a dissociation constant of 14.5 nmol/L. MK-0677 and EP 51389 did not displace it (Circ Res 1999).
- item: Identified as CD36 — description: Sequencing showed the protein was CD36. Hexarelin's coronary vasoconstrictor effect was absent in hearts from CD36-null mice (Circ Res 2002).
- item: GH-independent action — description: In hypophysectomized rats, which lack pituitary GH, hexarelin still protected isolated hearts from ischemia-reperfusion damage (Endocrinology 1999).

### subsection 4 — title: Desensitization
**items:**
- item: In cells — description: In cells expressing the human GHS-R1a, the calcium response to hexarelin desensitized within 2 to 5 minutes of a first exposure (J Endocrinol Invest 2003).
- item: In people — description: Repeated administration over 16 weeks in 12 healthy volunteers (described as elderly in the Growth Horm IGF Res report) partly reduced the GH response, which recovered 4 weeks after stopping (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1998; Growth Horm IGF Res 1998). Short intermittent courses in elderly subjects did not reduce it (Eur J Endocrinol 1996).

## Field: preclinicalDisclaimer

Animal and cell findings do not show safety or efficacy in people. Hexarelin is not approved. Research-market material sold under this name has not been evaluated by any regulator, and its identity, purity, and sterility are unverified.

## Field: preclinicalIntro

Preclinical work covers GH-axis pharmacology in rats and dogs, cardiovascular models in rats and mice, and pharmacokinetic studies. Many of these studies come from a small number of linked academic groups.

## Field: preclinicalSubsections

### subsection 1 — title: Discovery pharmacology (Life Sci 1994; PMID 7910650)
**findings:**
- title: Design — content: GH release after hexarelin or GHRP-6 was measured in conscious 10-day-old rats and in anesthetized adult male rats.
- title: Reported findings — content: Intravenous hexarelin and GHRP-6 produced similar prompt GH peaks within 10 minutes. Subcutaneous hexarelin produced a longer-lasting GH release and was slightly more effective than GHRP-6.
- title: Interpretation limits — content: Rodent GH responses to secretagogues do not predict human effects or safety.

### subsection 2 — title: Cardiac models in rats and mice
**findings:**
- title: Ischemia-reperfusion in rats — content: In hypophysectomized rats, a 7-day hexarelin course prevented the worsening of ischemia-reperfusion damage in explanted hearts. GH gave similar results, while EP 51389, a GH-releasing peptide that does not bind heart tissue, was ineffective (Endocrinology 1999; PMID 10465272).
- title: Rats after myocardial infarction — content: Two weeks of treatment, started four weeks after coronary ligation, increased stroke volume and cardiac output and lowered total peripheral resistance compared with saline in the higher of two hexarelin dose groups, similar to GH (Endocrinology 2000; PMID 10614623).
- title: Mice after myocardial infarction — content: 21 days of treatment after coronary ligation improved left-ventricular function on MRI at 14 days and reduced interstitial collagen compared with vehicle (Physiol Rep 2018; PMID 29756411).
- title: Interpretation limits — content: These are small animal studies, often from linked groups. No outcome trial has tested these effects in people.

### subsection 3 — title: Pharmacokinetics in animals
**findings:**
- title: Dogs — content: After intravenous administration, hexarelin had a terminal half-life of about 120 minutes (Peptides 1995; PMID 8545255).
- title: Rats — content: After intravenous administration, the half-life was 75.9 ± 9.3 minutes. Subcutaneous bioavailability was 64%, and biliary excretion was the main elimination route, with most excreted material unchanged (Drug Metab Dispos 2000; PMID 10611139).
- title: Interpretation limits — content: Animal pharmacokinetics do not establish a human half-life. No human plasma half-life for hexarelin itself was found in the sources checked.

## Field: humanUseIntro

Human data come from small physiology and diagnostic-testing studies published between 1994 and 2004, mostly by academic groups; developer staff led the 1994 dose-response study and co-authored many others. Most were single-administration studies. The longest ran for 16 weeks in adults, and up to 10 months in an uncontrolled study in children. None was a large randomized efficacy or outcome trial. ClinicalTrials.gov lists no registered hexarelin or examorelin studies (searched 8 Oct 2026). This page gives no dose amounts or schedules. Numbers from different studies are not directly comparable.

## Field: humanUseSubsections

### subsection 1 — title: Single-administration GH studies (1994–1995)
**entries:**
- type: content — value: **Routes compared (peer-reviewed; J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1994, PMID 8126144):** in 12 healthy young volunteers, intravenous hexarelin released about twice as much GH as GHRH at the same dose. Biological bioavailability relative to intravenous administration was 77.0% subcutaneous, 4.8% intranasal, and 0.3% oral.
- type: content — value: **Dose-response (peer-reviewed; Eur J Clin Pharmacol 1994, PMID 7957536):** in a double-blind, placebo-controlled, rising-dose study in 12 healthy men, GH rose dose-dependently, peaked at about 30 minutes, and returned to baseline within 240 minutes.
- type: content — value: **Children and pituitary disease (peer-reviewed; J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1995, PMID 7852535):** hexarelin caused clear GH peaks 15 to 30 minutes after injection in short normal children. It caused no GH increase in five subjects with organic hypopituitarism. The authors reported no adverse side effects in this single-administration study.
- type: item — value: These responses describe GH-axis physiology and diagnostic testing, not a treatment effect.

### subsection 2 — title: Endocrine effects beyond GH
**entries:**
- type: content — value: **ACTH and cortisol (peer-reviewed; J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1997, PMID 9253314):** in 12 normal subjects, ACTH and cortisol responses to hexarelin were similar to those after human corticotropin-releasing hormone. In 17 patients with Cushing's syndrome, the response depended on the cause, with a strongly enhanced response in pituitary Cushing's disease.
- type: content — value: **Dose-response for other hormones (peer-reviewed; J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1996, PMID 8954038):** prolactin and cortisol rose alongside GH in healthy adult men.
- type: content — value: **Sleep (peer-reviewed; Psychoneuroendocrinology 2004, PMID 15177700):** in seven young volunteers, repeated night-time administration reduced stage 4 sleep and EEG delta power and increased GH, prolactin, ACTH, and cortisol.

### subsection 3 — title: Repeated and longer-term administration
**entries:**
- type: content — value: **16 weeks of repeated administration (peer-reviewed; J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1998, PMID 9589671; Growth Horm IGF Res 1998, PMID 10990150):** in 12 healthy volunteers, described as elderly in the Growth Horm IGF Res abstract, the GH response to hexarelin fell significantly by weeks 4 and 16 and recovered 4 weeks after stopping. IGF-I, IGFBP-3, total body fat, lean body mass, and bone mineral density did not change significantly.
- type: content — value: **Cortisol in the same 16-week regimen (peer-reviewed; Clin Endocrinol 1999, PMID 10341859):** the cortisol response decreased significantly after 16 weeks and was close to baseline 4 weeks after stopping.
- type: content — value: **Short intermittent courses (peer-reviewed; Eur J Endocrinol 1996, PMID 8921821):** 8-day intranasal and 15-day oral courses in elderly subjects did not reduce the GH response, and no side effects were reported.
- type: content — value: **Short children (peer-reviewed, uncontrolled; Clin Endocrinol 1995, PMID 8548949):** in 8 prepubertal short children given intranasal hexarelin for up to 8 months, mean growth velocity rose from 5.3 to 8.3 cm/year and IGF-I increased. A related study from the same group in 7 children over 6 to 10 months found partial desensitization of the GH response while growth velocity still increased (Eur J Endocrinol 1996, PMID 8766941). Neither study had a control group.
- type: item — value: Small, uncontrolled, or single-group studies cannot establish efficacy or long-term safety.

### subsection 4 — title: Single-administration cardiac studies
**entries:**
- type: content — value: **Healthy men (peer-reviewed; J Endocrinol Invest 1999, PMID 10342360):** in 7 male volunteers, hexarelin raised left-ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) from 64.0% to 70.7% for up to 60 minutes. Recombinant GH that produced similar GH levels did not change LVEF.
- type: content — value: **GH deficiency (peer-reviewed; Eur J Pharmacol 1999, PMID 10528131):** in 7 men with GH deficiency, LVEF rose after hexarelin even though their GH response was negligible.
- type: content — value: **Heart disease (peer-reviewed; Eur J Heart Fail 2002, PMID 11959048; Eur J Pharmacol 2002, PMID 12144941):** in 13 patients with severe left-ventricular dysfunction, LVEF rose in ischemic but not in dilated cardiomyopathy. In a study of 24 men undergoing coronary bypass surgery, split across hexarelin, GHRH, recombinant GH, and placebo groups, hexarelin increased LVEF, cardiac index, and cardiac output, while GHRH, recombinant GH, and placebo did not.
- type: item — value: These were single-administration hemodynamic studies. No trial tested clinical outcomes such as heart failure events.

### subsection 5 — title: Registry status and safety themes
**entries:**
- type: content — value: ClinicalTrials.gov returned no studies for "hexarelin", "examorelin", or "EP-23905" on 8 Oct 2026. Most human papers were published in the 1990s. ClinicalTrials.gov registration began in 2000, after most of these studies.
- type: content — value: Effects reported in these short studies center on the endocrine profile: increases in cortisol, ACTH, and prolactin, a lower GH response after repeated exposure, and less deep sleep in one study. Long-term safety data in people were not found in the sources checked.
- type: item — value: This page gives no administration, dose, or self-use information.

## Field: potentialApplications

- title: GH-axis testing — description: Studied as a stimulus of pituitary GH secretion in healthy volunteers, children, and people with pituitary disease (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1994; 1995).
- title: HPA-axis research — description: Trial authors suggested it could help distinguish pituitary from ectopic ACTH-dependent Cushing's syndrome (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 1997).
- title: Short stature — description: Small uncontrolled intranasal studies in prepubertal children in the mid-1990s.
- title: Cardiac contractility and protection — description: Single-administration human hemodynamic studies and animal ischemia and infarction models.

## Field: areasOfResearchIntro

The open questions are how much of hexarelin's action runs through CD36 rather than GHS-R1a, whether the short-lived cardiac effects mean anything clinically, and how the receptor adapts to repeated exposure.

## Field: areasOfResearch

- name: CD36 signalling — description: CD36 binding links hexarelin to cardiac, vascular, and lipid-metabolism research (Circ Res 2002; review in J Geriatr Cardiol 2014).
- name: GH-independent cardiac effects — description: Supported by small single-administration human studies and animal models. No outcome trials.
- name: Receptor desensitization — description: Rapid desensitization in cells; partial, reversible attenuation in people.
- name: Non-selective endocrine effects — description: Prolactin, ACTH, and cortisol release, including a strongly enhanced ACTH response in pituitary Cushing's disease.
- name: Anti-doping detection — description: Urine methods detect hexarelin and metabolites such as hexarelin (1-3) free acid after the parent peptide is no longer found (Drug Test Anal 2015).

## Field: keyPoints

- Synthetic GHRP-6 analog (INN examorelin; CAS 140703-51-1). Not approved by any regulator found in the sources checked.
- Acts on the ghrelin receptor (GHS-R1a) to release GH, and also binds CD36 in heart tissue.
- Not GH-selective: human studies recorded prolactin, ACTH, and cortisol increases.
- Repeated administration produced a partial, reversible fall in the GH response in a 16-week study.
- Human evidence consists of small physiology studies from 1994 to 2004. ClinicalTrials.gov lists no registered studies.
- The WADA 2026 and 2027 Prohibited Lists name examorelin (hexarelin) under S2.2.4, prohibited at all times.

## Field: conclusion

Hexarelin is a research peptide with a substantial 1990s literature from the GHRP program. Peer-reviewed human studies document its GH-releasing pharmacology, its effects on prolactin, ACTH, and cortisol, and partial desensitization with repeated use. Its binding to CD36 gave it a separate role in cardiovascular research. No approval, marketing authorization, or registered trial was found in the sources checked, and it is prohibited in sport. ## FAQs

**Is hexarelin the same as GHRP-6?** No. Hexarelin is a GHRP-6 analog that differs by one methyl group on the second residue (2-methyl-D-tryptophan in place of D-tryptophan). It has its own INN (examorelin), CAS number, and literature, so data for GHRP-6 do not automatically apply to hexarelin.

**Is hexarelin approved?** No. As of 8 Oct 2026 no FDA or EU approval was found, and ClinicalTrials.gov lists no registered hexarelin studies. The published human studies from 1994 to 2004 are research, not approval.

**Is hexarelin banned in sport?** Yes. The WADA 2026 and 2027 Prohibited Lists name examorelin (hexarelin) among GH-releasing peptides under section S2.2.4, which covers substances prohibited at all times.

**Does hexarelin only raise growth hormone?** No. Human studies found that it also raises prolactin, ACTH, and cortisol, and in healthy adults its ACTH and cortisol effect was similar in size to that of corticotropin-releasing hormone. It also binds CD36, a receptor linked to cardiac effects seen in animal studies and small single-dose human studies.

**Does the response to hexarelin fade with repeated use?** Partly. In a 16-week study reported in two 1998 papers, the GH response fell and then recovered about 4 weeks after stopping. Short intermittent courses did not reduce the response. Cell studies show the receptor can desensitize within minutes.
