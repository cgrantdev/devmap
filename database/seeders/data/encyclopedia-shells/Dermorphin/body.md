## Field: overviewShort

**Dermorphin** is a naturally occurring **heptapeptide opioid** first isolated from the skin of South American frogs of the genus *Phyllomedusa*. Sequence: **H-Tyr-D-Ala-Phe-Gly-Tyr-Pro-Ser-NH₂**. It is a highly potent, selective **μ-opioid receptor (MOR)** agonist in classic pharmacology assays. It is **not FDA-approved** as a medicine. Educational only—not medical advice; **not a dosing guide**.

## Field: overview

Dermorphin is famous in peptide chemistry for containing a **D-alanine** at position 2—unusual among vertebrate peptides and essential for activity. Popular and grey-market interest sometimes frames it as a “natural super-opioid”; encyclopedia priority is accurate pharmacology literacy, clear preclinical vs clinical limits, and **controlled-substance / diversion risk awareness**. Marketplace powders are not approved analgesics.

## Field: background

Isolated and sequenced around 1981 from *Phyllomedusa sauvagei* skin extracts (Montecucchi; Broccardo et al.). The precursor is ribosomally synthesized with a standard L-alanine codon; the D-Ala arises by **post-translational stereoinversion**. Related amphibian opioids (deltorphins) show δ-preferring pharmacology—do not conflate.

## Field: mechanismOfActionIntro

Dermorphin acts primarily as a **μ-opioid receptor agonist**, producing classic opioid pharmacology in isolated tissue and animal nociception models.

Animal analgesic potency (often summarized as “many-fold vs morphine” in historical reviews) is **not** a human dosing conversion and does not authorize recreational or veterinary grey-market use. Opioid class risks (respiratory depression, dependence, diversion) apply in principle.

## FAQs

**Is dermorphin a human hormone?** No—amphibian skin peptide (not endogenous in humans).

**Why D-alanine?** Post-translational stereoinversion; required for activity.

**FDA-approved pain drug?** No.

**Safer than morphine because “natural”?** No—μ-opioid pharmacology still carries class risks.

**Same as deltorphin?** No—related frog opioids with different receptor preferences.
